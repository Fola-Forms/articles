A federal judge blocked the Trump administration from implementing a rule that would limit how long foreign students and journalists can stay in the U.S. without applying for extensions, with U.S. District Judge F. Dennis Saylor in Boston ruling in favor of a coalition of unions and higher education advocacy groups a day before the Department of Homeland Security's rule was set to take effect. The preliminary injunction halts a major policy shift in how the government regulates international student and journalist visa duration.

## What changed

The rule DHS adopted in July sought to replace the current "duration of status" system with one that would substantially restrict the overall number of foreign students, professors and journalists in the country, with F visas for international students and J visas for cultural exchange programs capped at four years, while I visas for journalists would be limited to 240 days. The rule would have eliminated the "duration of status" system that has been in place for more than 40 years.

Judge F Dennis Saylor, a US district judge in Boston, ruled in favour of a coalition of unions and higher education advocacy groups a day before the federal Department of Homeland Security's (DHS) rule was set to take effect.

Judge Saylor rejected the government's legal justification for the rule on multiple grounds. The judge said the rationale for the restriction was "exceptionally weak", and the connection between the rule and the problems it purported to address was "exceptionally attenuated," noting that DHS had built the case "almost entirely on a small handful of anecdotes, each of which involved incidents that the new rule would do nothing to prevent or even mitigate."

The judge ruled that the new policy would probably cause "catastrophic" damage to the US economy and higher-education system, rejecting the government's claims that it was needed to protect the country. The judge questioned whether the real purpose was not to safeguard national security and protect borders, but to achieve other undisclosed goals such as asserting greater government control over academic institutions and the press.

The court found that the government had not adequately considered the possibility that journalists who file critical coverage could be denied extensions, the resulting self-censorship, or the risk of retaliation by foreign governments.

## Why it matters

The preliminary injunction means the "duration of status" system is preserved, protecting 1.6 million F visas and 500,000 J visas. This is critical for international student and scholar admissions and status maintenance going forward.

For practitioners advising F-1, J-1, and I-visa clients, you can continue to rely on the longstanding "duration of status" framework—students remain lawfully in status for the entire duration of their program (or authorized employment/assignment), not a fixed calendar date. You do not need to advise clients that they must apply for extension requests on arbitrary four-year or 240-day clocks.

Universities and employers relying on international talent also avoid the administrative burden that would have come with the new extension requirements under the DHS rule. The judge wrote that the rule upended a system through which the U.S. for nearly five decades had issued visas to foreign students for their "duration of status," which has permitted tens of millions of foreign students and researchers to come to the U.S., leading to groundbreaking research in science, medicine, and technology, substantial economic growth, and other benefits.

However, this ruling is a preliminary injunction, not a final decision on the merits. The case will continue through the courts, and the government may appeal.

## Way forward

- **Verify current guidance**: Ensure all your client advisories on F-1, J-1, and I-visa duration reflect the reinstatement of the "duration of status" standard. Do not counsel clients to file preemptive extension applications based on the now-blocked rule.

- **Track appellate developments**: DHS or the Department of Justice may appeal Judge Saylor's ruling. Monitor federal appellate court filings in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the First Circuit to assess the government's next move and timeline.

- **Document the injunction date**: For new F-1 and J-1 admissions after September 15, 2026, confirm that clients' I-94 records reflect "D/S" (Duration of Status) in the departure field, not a fixed date.

- **Prepare for possible reversal**: While the preliminary injunction is favorable, the merits litigation is ongoing. Develop contingency advice for clients in case a higher court reverses this decision or the rule is ultimately upheld in modified form.
