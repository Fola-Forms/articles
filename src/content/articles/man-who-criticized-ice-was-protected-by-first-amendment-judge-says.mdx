A federal judge in Washington DC has blocked the Department of Homeland Security from issuing official "warning notices" that threaten criminal prosecution against US citizens for criticizing Immigration and Customs Enforcement. The ruling protects First Amendment rights and establishes an important boundary on how federal agencies can respond to political speech directed at immigration officials.

## What changed

Federal Judge Rudolph Contreras ruled on Tuesday that the Department of Homeland Security may no longer issue a "warning notice" – threatening prosecution against a US citizen – for criticizing Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE). The ruling sided with David Streever, a New York man who accused DHS officials of violating his first amendment rights when they showed up first at his home in June with a "warning notice", claiming he made a threat against former ICE director Todd Lyons, and then to a hotel where Streever and his young daughter were staying.

The notice and visits were in response to an email Streever sent Lyons in January, after federal agents fatally shot two US citizens amid an immigration crackdown in Minnesota. Judge Contreras found that Streever was likely to prevail on his First Amendment claim, stating the notice "practically shouts" that Streever could face prosecution if he continued his political speech, and in the September 15 order temporarily vacated the notice and barred the defendants from relying on either the notice or Streever's January email in future prosecutions or investigations.

The Foundation for Individual Rights and Expression (Fire), a non-profit first amendment advocacy group, represented Streever in his lawsuit.

## Why it matters

This ruling is significant for practitioners advising clients on immigration-related activism and advocacy. Streever argued that the notice and attempts to track him down caused him to censor his criticism of ICE—a dynamic known as the "chilling effect" on free speech. By blocking DHS from issuing warning notices for criticism of immigration enforcement, Judge Contreras has protected not only Streever's rights but also those of other citizens and advocates who speak out on immigration policy.

The decision signals that federal agencies cannot use investigative machinery and official warning notices as a pretext for silencing political dissent. Judge Contreras enforced that First Amendment boundary against the Trump administration's use of federal investigators and a criminal warning notice in response to Streever's protected criticism. For practitioners working with clients engaged in lawful advocacy—whether letters to officials, public testimony, or media commentary—this ruling establishes that such speech cannot be the sole or primary basis for federal retaliation.

## Way forward

- **Document any contact from DHS or HSI related to speech or advocacy**: If a client receives a warning notice, investigative letter, or official contact referencing criticism of ICE or other immigration enforcement, preserve that correspondence and consult with civil rights counsel immediately.

- **Understand the scope of protection**: First Amendment-protected speech—including emails to officials, public criticism, and advocacy—cannot be the basis for threatened prosecution, warning notices, or investigation under this ruling. Counsel clients that such lawful speech is protected even if they mention federal officials or policy changes.

- **Reference this ruling in correspondence**: If DHS or HSI initiates contact with a client regarding protected speech, practitioners can now cite Judge Contreras's order to establish that the agency is violating established law by continuing such investigations.

- **Monitor the pending case**: Defendants must answer Streever's complaint by October 5, 2026, while Judge Contreras considers the remaining constitutional claims. The full outcome of *Streever v. Mullin* may yield additional protections.
