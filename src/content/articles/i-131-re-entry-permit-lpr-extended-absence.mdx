A lawful permanent resident who plans an extended absence from the United States has one tool to preserve status against an abandonment finding at the port of entry: a **[re-entry permit on Form I-131](https://www.uscis.gov/i-131)**. The permit is valid for two years from issuance and rebuts the presumption of abandonment that arises from an absence of one year or more. It does not, however, restart the clock for [naturalization continuous residence](/articles/n-400-statutory-period-continuous-residence), and it does not stop a CBP officer from finding abandonment on facts independent of the absence itself. The sequence — file first, biometrics second, then depart — is the part that traps the most LPRs.

## What changed

Re-entry permits are governed by [INA §223](https://www.uscis.gov/laws/immigration-and-nationality-act) and [8 CFR 223.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-8/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-223). The current operating framework reflects three guidance documents:

- **[USCIS Policy Manual Vol. 11, Part D, Chapter 2](https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-11-part-d-chapter-2)** consolidates re-entry permit adjudication standards and supersedes legacy field memoranda.
- **The [USCIS instructions to Form I-131](https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/document/forms/i-131instr.pdf)** specify that the application must be **filed while the applicant is physically present in the United States**, and that **biometrics must be captured before departure** at an [Application Support Center](https://www.uscis.gov/about-us/find-a-uscis-office/field-offices).
- **[8 CFR 223.2(c)(2)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-8/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-223/section-223.2)** caps the validity of a re-entry permit for an LPR who has been outside the U.S. for four of the last five years (since becoming an LPR) at **one year**, not two — a trap for serial filers.

## Why it matters

The CBP officer at the port of entry has two questions when an LPR returns after a long absence:

1. **Does the LPR have a valid travel document?** A green card alone is treated as a valid travel document for absences of less than one year. For absences of one year or more, the LPR needs either a re-entry permit OR an [SB-1 returning resident visa](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/immigrate/returning-resident.html) issued by a U.S. consulate. A re-entry permit valid on the date of return defeats the "no document" objection.
2. **Did the LPR intend to abandon residence?** The permit does NOT answer this question — it merely shifts the burden. [Matter of Kane, 15 I&N Dec. 258 (BIA 1975)](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/page/file/1118801/dl) and its progeny look to family ties, U.S. tax filings, ownership of a U.S. residence, U.S. employment, and the reason for the absence. An LPR who closes the U.S. apartment, moves the spouse and children abroad, stops filing U.S. taxes as a resident, and takes a foreign job is at significant abandonment risk even WITH a re-entry permit.

The interaction with naturalization is the second trap. [INA §316(a)](https://www.uscis.gov/laws/immigration-and-nationality-act) requires continuous residence. An absence of more than six months but less than one year creates a [rebuttable presumption of break in continuous residence](https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-12-part-d-chapter-2); an absence of one year or more breaks it absolutely — and a re-entry permit does NOT change that. An LPR who is preserving status with a re-entry permit may need to restart the [naturalization physical presence](/articles/n-400-physical-presence-rule) clock entirely. Preservation of LPR status and preservation of naturalization eligibility are different problems with different tools — the [N-470 application to preserve continuous residence](https://www.uscis.gov/n-470) is the right tool for the latter, and only for narrow categories (U.S. employer assignments, religious organizations, certain research positions).

## Way forward

For the LPR planning an extended absence:

- **File the [Form I-131](https://www.uscis.gov/i-131) at least 60 days before the planned biometrics appointment, which itself must precede departure.** The current filing fee is $630 plus $85 biometrics — see the [USCIS filing fees page](https://www.uscis.gov/forms/filing-fees). The Texas Service Center processes most re-entry permits.
- **Plan around the biometrics window.** Biometrics notices typically issue within 4 to 8 weeks of filing. If the planned departure is sooner, file a [Form I-131A "Travel Document Application"](https://www.uscis.gov/i-131a) at a consulate after departure ONLY if the original re-entry permit was approved before departure but was lost or damaged — it is NOT a substitute for the original I-131. File early.
- **Request early or split biometrics if needed** by contacting the [USCIS Contact Center](https://www.uscis.gov/contactcenter) once the receipt notice arrives. Practitioners report mixed success; the safer plan is to delay departure.
- **Maintain U.S. ties during the absence.** Keep a U.S. address, file [Form 1040](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-1040) as a resident (not 1040-NR), keep U.S. bank accounts, maintain a U.S. driver's license if possible, and document the reason for the absence (employment letter, school enrollment, family medical situation). CBP secondary inspection will ask.

For the LPR who has already departed without a permit:

- **If the absence is approaching one year**, return to the U.S. now and file the I-131 from inside the country before re-departing. This is the cleanest fix.
- **If the absence has exceeded one year**, the I-131 is no longer available — the LPR must apply for an [SB-1 returning resident visa](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/immigrate/returning-resident.html) at the consulate, which requires showing that the prolonged stay was caused by reasons beyond the LPR's control. SB-1 grants are not guaranteed.
