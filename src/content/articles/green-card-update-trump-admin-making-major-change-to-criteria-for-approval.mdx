The Trump administration has imposed a series of restrictive policy changes taking effect in July 2026 that narrow green card approval standards. The changes combine tighter visa availability, stricter technical review of applications, and a reinforced discretionary approach to adjustment of status that affects how USCIS officers evaluate eligibility.

## What changed

The State Department's July 2026 Visa Bulletin implements new visa limits alongside a federal rule that expands the government's authority to reject or deny applications over technical errors, accompanied by broader policy guidance emphasizing stricter case-by-case review of residency requests.

Visas in the employment-based second preference (EB-2) category for applicants from India, along with unreserved EB-5 investor visas from the same country, are now unavailable for the remainder of the fiscal year, meaning no further approvals can be issued until October.

Federal officials have said the change is intended to standardize enforcement of filing requirements, but it increases the consequences of administrative errors for applicants, who may be required to restart the process with a new application and pay a new fee.

The changes reflect a May policy memorandum from USCIS that reinforces the discretionary nature of adjustment of status, where the agency described adjustment of status as a discretionary form of relief and directed officers to evaluate cases individually, weighing a broad range of factors beyond basic eligibility.

## Why it matters

You need to recalibrate your filing and interview strategy. While applicants who meet statutory requirements may still qualify, approval is not guaranteed, and immigration attorneys say the guidance has contributed to greater uncertainty and closer scrutiny in some cases.

The EB-2 India visa availability shutdown is particularly consequential for employment-based practitioners. The July bulletin shows mounting pressure in employment-based immigration, with annual caps reached in some high-demand categories. Applicants will face a four-month hold until the October 1 fiscal year reset.

The technical-error rule raises the stakes for application completeness. Any missing document, wrong date, or procedural misstep now carries the risk of outright denial and forced re-filing, rather than a request for evidence (RFE) allowing you to cure defects.

The discretionary adjustment of status standard means you cannot rely solely on statutory eligibility anymore. Officers will consider factors beyond the legal requirements, and approval is no longer a routine matter. This requires more robust case building and interview preparation.

## Way forward

- **For EB-2 India applicants**: Do not file I-485 or Plan B consular processing until the visa cap resets October 1. Work with your client to maintain current status and employment authorization during the freeze.

- **For all applicants**: Request evidence of eligibility *before* filing. Confirm all biographical data, work history, educational credentials, and financial documents with your client; do not assume USCIS will issue an RFE.

- **For adjustment of status filers**: Prepare a supplemental statement explaining why adjustment of status is appropriate in your client's particular circumstances—family ties, employment disruption risk, or other equitable factors—beyond bare statutory eligibility.

- **Monitor the visa bulletin**: Check the [State Department's Visa Bulletin](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/legal/visa-law0/visa-statistics/visa-bulletin.html) monthly; additional category freezes may occur before September 30.
