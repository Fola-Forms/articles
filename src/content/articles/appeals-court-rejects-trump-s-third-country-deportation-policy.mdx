The Boston-based 1st U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals rejected on Friday a Trump administration policy that allows authorities to rapidly deport migrants to countries other than their own without giving them a chance to raise safety concerns, largely upholding a February decision by a lower-court judge that declared the Department of Homeland Security's policy unlawful. This appellate ruling significantly impacts how immigration practitioners advise clients facing removal and shapes the legal landscape heading into potential Supreme Court review.

## What changed

The ruling came in a class-action lawsuit filed by migrants subject to deportation orders that has become a key test of what due process protections the government must provide before removing people to countries with which they have no connection. The Trump administration had twice before in the same case convinced the Supreme Court to intervene after Murphy had ruled against it in earlier phases of the litigation over the third-country policy, and it had indicated a willingness to go back to the high court again if the 1st Circuit upheld Murphy's latest ruling.

However, the 1st Circuit overturned one part of Murphy's ruling on procedural grounds, concerning whether the government must first try to deport migrants to countries they have ties to before sending them to third countries. 

Under the policy, migrants can be deported to such countries if immigration authorities have credible diplomatic assurances they will not be persecuted or tortured there, or alternatively, authorities can proceed if they have given migrants as little as six hours of notice that they are being sent to such a country. The appellate panel's decision preserves the core due-process requirement that migrants receive written notice and an opportunity to be heard before removal to an unanticipated third country.

## Why it matters

The 1st Circuit's decision establishes binding precedent in its jurisdiction that the government must provide migrants due process protections before removing them to countries they have no connection to. For immigration practitioners, this means:

- **Notice requirement**: Practitioners can now cite controlling authority that the government must provide written notice of a third-country deportation before it occurs, not six hours beforehand or after the fact.
- **Opportunity to be heard**: Clients have a legally protected opportunity to raise claims of potential persecution, torture, or other harm tied to the receiving country.
- **Scope of clients affected**: The administration has entered into a series of agreements that have allowed it to send more than 25,000 migrants to at least 29 third countries, in many cases Mexico. The ruling protects a potentially large class of affected individuals.
- **Supreme Court trajectory**: The Trump administration had twice before convinced the Supreme Court to intervene and indicated a willingness to go back to the high court again if the 1st Circuit upheld Murphy's latest ruling. Practitioners should anticipate emergency motions and potential stays as the case climbs to the Supreme Court.

## Way forward

- **Immediate**: Review any pending client files involving removal orders and assess whether a third-country deportation is threatened. If so, file a motion to preserve due-process rights or seek an administrative stay pending notice and opportunity to be heard.
- **Litigation strategy**: Cite this 1st Circuit decision in any motion or brief challenging a third-country deportation within the circuit's jurisdiction. The precedent is now established at the appellate level.
- **Client communication**: Advise clients facing removal that federal courts have now confirmed they have a legal right to written notice and a hearing before deportation to a non-designated country. Do not assume administrative assurances satisfy this standard.
- **Monitor Supreme Court docket**: Watch for emergency petitions or appeals by the Department of Justice seeking stays or review of this 1st Circuit decision; the case is likely to return to SCOTUS.
