On Friday, September 12, the Supreme Court dismissed a major immigration case addressing whether certain detained noncitizens have a constitutional right to a bond hearing. But the government signaled it will pursue the same issue again—possibly this term.

## What changed

The Supreme Court dismissed Genalo v. Black, which had been scheduled for oral argument in October and addressed whether certain noncitizens have a right to a bond hearing during a lengthy detention and what the government must show to justify continued detention if such a hearing is required.

The case involved two noncitizens detained under Section 1226(c) of the Immigration and Nationality Act, which requires the detention of noncitizens, including green card holders, who have committed certain crimes and who are awaiting removal proceedings. The Court dismissed it as moot after New York Gov. Kathy Hochul pardoned Keisy Guerrero Mariano in late August.

However, the Solicitor General agreed the case is now moot, but said the Trump administration will seek an "alternative vehicle" to challenge the Second Circuit's 2024 ruling and intends to find another case through which the Supreme Court can resolve the issue during its 2026-27 term.

## Why it matters

This dismissal does not end the fight over a noncitizen's right to a bond hearing. The Second Circuit ruled that due process requires an individualized bond hearing when detention becomes unreasonably prolonged, and in Black's case, the court also upheld a requirement that the government prove by clear and convincing evidence that continued detention was necessary. The federal government is asking the Supreme Court to overturn that ruling.

If you represent a noncitizen detained under Section 1226(c)—a provision that mandates detention for certain individuals with criminal convictions and provides no time limit—you face uncertainty about what rights your client has to a bond hearing. The Second Circuit says prolonged detention triggers due-process protections. The Trump administration disagrees and is actively seeking another case to overrule that precedent.

The dismissal buys time but does not resolve the split among courts of appeals or provide Supreme Court guidance.

## Way forward

- **Monitor the docket.** Watch https://www.supremecourt.gov/ and SCOTUSblog for the government's next petition on this issue, likely within the next few weeks.
- **Preserve the record in Section 1226(c) cases.** If you are currently representing someone detained under this provision, brief the facts about the length of detention, changed circumstances, and the lack of any individualized review. Document any assertion that detention is justified.
- **Brief the Second Circuit law.** Until the Supreme Court rules, the Second Circuit's holding in this case applies in its circuit. Cite it if your case falls within that jurisdiction.
- **Prepare for the return.** The government has signaled this is not over. A new petition could arrive by the start of the 2026–27 Supreme Court term (October). Have contingent arguments ready.
