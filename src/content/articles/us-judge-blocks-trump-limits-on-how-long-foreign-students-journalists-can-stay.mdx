A federal judge on Monday blocked U.S. President Donald Trump's administration from implementing a new rule that would limit how long foreign students and journalists can remain in the U.S. without applying for extensions, with U.S. District Judge F. Dennis Saylor in Boston ruling in favor of a coalition of unions and higher education advocacy groups a day before the Department of Homeland Security's rule was set to take effect.

## What changed

DHS published a Final Rule on July 17, 2026, that would have replaced the longstanding "duration of status" framework for F-1 students, J-1 exchange visitors, and I visa holders with fixed periods of admission. Under the rule, F-1 and J-1 visa holders would generally have been limited to four-year admission periods, and I visa holders (foreign media representatives) would have been limited to 240-day admission periods. The rule was scheduled to take effect on September 15, 2026.

Judge Saylor said DHS adopted the policy based on "exceptionally weak" rationales, and while the agency had cited national security and the need to prevent fraud in the visa program, the judge found it had not adhered to its legal obligations to engage with concerns about a policy change or consider less burdensome alternatives.

On September 14, 2026, the U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts issued a nationwide preliminary injunction postponing the effective date of the Department of Homeland Security's (DHS) Final Rule that would have fundamentally changed admission periods for F-1 students, J-1 exchange visitors and I media representatives. As a result of the court's order, the current duration of status (D/S) framework remains in place while litigation continues.

## Why it matters

About 1.6 million people currently have F visas and another 500,000 have J visas. For international students pursuing multi-year degree programs (especially graduate research), a four-year cap would have forced mid-program extensions of stay, creating administrative burden and uncertainty. Major research universities like Massachusetts Institute of Technology and Harvard have large percentages of foreign students, particularly at the graduate level; if the rule took effect, such universities would likely suffer hundreds of millions of dollars in costs and enrollment would decline, with Saylor writing "The damage to the higher education system and to the economy of the United States is likely to be catastrophic."

For immigration counsel advising international students and exchange visitors: the duration-of-status framework remains in effect while the litigation continues. The rule will not take effect unless and until further court action allows it to move forward. This blocking order applies nationwide, so the current I-20 and DS-2019 admission model continues unchanged for now.

## Way forward

- **Monitor the litigation:** This is a preliminary injunction, not a final ruling. DHS may appeal, or the merits case may proceed. Check the District of Massachusetts docket (case No. 1:26-cv-13799) and SEVIS/SEVP announcements for updates.
- **Continue advising under D/S:** Until the rule takes effect (if ever), counsel students and scholars on standard duration-of-status doctrine: admit for the program length with no fixed I-94 expiration, and ensure clients maintain status by following I-20/DS-2019 terms.
- **Prepare contingency guidance:** If the injunction is later lifted or modified, have a plan to brief clients on extension-of-stay filing deadlines and cap deadlines.
- **Watch for appeal:** If DHS files a notice of appeal or seeks a stay of the injunction, the timeline could accelerate. Subscribe to alerts from SEVP or your professional association for immediate notice.
