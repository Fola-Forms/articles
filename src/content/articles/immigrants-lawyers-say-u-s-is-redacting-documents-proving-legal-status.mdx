Immigrants seeking documents from the government to prove that they are in the country legally are being rebuffed or provided paperwork that is heavily redacted, according to dozens of immigration lawyers, who say the refusals put their clients at risk of deportation. The change can likely be traced to a 90-page legal memorandum that the Trump administration published in early March, which discussed one specific exemption used to justify the withholding and redacting of information for FOIA requests: the "(b)(6)" exemption.

## What changed

Redactions and withholdings had averaged just 10% between May 2024 and April 2025. Since April 2025, however, the rate has increased dramatically. In the past, federal agencies routinely turned over documents immigrants requested — for instance, when international adoptees sought proof of their parents' U.S. citizenship, or noncitizens wanted copies of petitions or interview transcripts for amnesty programs.

The (b)(6) exemption, which now makes up the vast majority of records withheld from clients, applies to "personnel and medical files and similar files" when disclosure of such information "would constitute a clearly unwarranted invasion of personal privacy." The government has sometimes asserted that it could not find even documents that immigrants and their lawyers know exist, such as an expired passport or parts of prior applications.

## Why it matters

When immigrants apply for relief, seek to defend against removal, or pursue derivative citizenship claims, they often need government-held records as evidence. FOIA responses that arrive heavily redacted or with documents withheld leave your clients unable to prove the facts they need:

- **Proof of prior legal status** for cancellation of removal, U-visa, T-visa, or VAWA applications
- **Evidence of approved petitions or notices** showing that family or employer sponsorships were granted
- **Interview transcripts and prior applications** that may contain admissions or context helpful to current claims
- **Passport and travel documents** that establish entry dates, periods of stay, or nationality

"If you hide the information from immigrants and you have lousy lawyers filing documentation for them, they're going to be deported," according to an immigration lawyer and former ICE prosecutor. A practitioner without access to these records may inadvertently make inconsistent factual assertions or miss corroborating evidence that a well-resourced opponent (DHS counsel) already possesses.

## Way forward

- **FOIA requests must specify why records are needed.** Frame requests to address privacy concerns head-on. For example, if you seek a record about your own client, argue that your client has consented to disclosure and that the privacy interest is minimal where the subject of the record is the requester themselves.

- **Consider FOIA appeals.** If DHS redacts or withholds records, file a detailed appeal within 20 business days explaining why the (b)(6) exemption does not apply or why the public interest in disclosure outweighs privacy concerns (a mandatory balancing test).

- **Cite the primary source.** Reference <https://gvwire.com/2026/09/14/immigrants-lawyers-say-us-is-redacting-documents-proving-legal-status/> when raising this issue with immigration judges, the BIA, or reviewing courts. Courts have held that agency practices in FOIA administration can be arbitrary and capricious if applied inconsistently.

- **Request a Vaughn Index.** If DHS withholds records, demand a detailed agency declaration (a "Vaughn Index") that itemizes each withheld document, identifies the exemption, and explains why disclosure would cause harm. This gives you a roadmap for appeal.
