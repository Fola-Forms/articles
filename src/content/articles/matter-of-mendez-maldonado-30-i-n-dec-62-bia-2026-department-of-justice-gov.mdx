The Board of Immigration Appeals issued Matter of Mendez Maldonado, 30 I&N Dec. 62 (BIA 2026) on September 22, 2026. The decision overrules two of the oldest BIA precedents in immigration practice: Matter of J-, 2 I&N Dec. 285 (BIA 1945), which is 81 years old, and Matter of K-, 7 I&N Dec. 594 (BIA 1957), which is 69 years old. This landmark ruling eliminates a critical interview safeguard that has protected noncitizens for nearly a century.

## What changed

The two overruled cases required government officers to explain the elements of a criminal offense to a noncitizen before eliciting a statement that could be used against them under the inadmissibility grounds of the Immigration and Nationality Act. The BIA has now held that no such warning is required.

This means USCIS officers conducting adjustment-of-status interviews, naturalization interviews, and other benefit interviews no longer have a legal obligation to warn applicants before asking questions that might elicit incriminating responses. Statements made without warning can now be used as grounds of inadmissibility under the INA.

## Why it matters

If you have a USCIS interview coming up for adjustment of status, for naturalization, or for any other benefit that requires a sworn interview, the safeguards that limited what a USCIS officer could do with your statements have narrowed. 

For practitioners, this decision fundamentally changes your approach to interview preparation and client counseling:

- **Client vulnerability has increased.** Your client no longer has the protection of a mandatory warning at the USCIS interview table. Any statement about criminal history, prior entries, prior removals, or other conduct can be used against them.
- **Strategic silence is now critical.** Without a warning requirement, the burden falls entirely on your client to understand which questions pose a trap. Many clients will not recognize the danger until it is too late.
- **Removal grounds now lurk in standard questions.** USCIS questions about travel, employment, and family history can now produce statements used to support grounds of inadmissibility or removability.

## Way forward

- **Brief every client before every USCIS interview.** Do not rely on the officer to explain what statements could be used against them. Review the specific factual vulnerabilities in your client's case—prior entries, criminal history, fraud, or family-based admissions—and rehearse how your client should respond.
- **Consider a written proffer to USCIS.** For clients with significant exposure, submit a brief describing the relevant facts before the interview and request that the interview proceed based on that record, rather than through live questioning.
- **Attend the interview if permitted.** Verify that you are permitted to attend under your representation rules (often dependent on the form and jurisdiction) and be present to interject if questions move into dangerous territory.
- **Document the officer's conduct.** If an officer asks questions knowing they may produce self-incriminatory statements without warning, note the exact questions and responses for any future appeal or motion.
