On September 18, 2026, President Trump issued an executive order entitled Enhancing Program Integrity and Interagency Coordination in the Administration of the H-1B Nonimmigrant Visa Program, coupled with a proclamation that extends the $100,000 fee for eligible H-1B petitions. Together, these documents introduce layoff-based scrutiny into H-1B adjudication and prolong a fee requirement that remains blocked by federal court order.

## What changed

The proclamation extends for another year the controversial $100,000 payment requirement for certain H-1B cases, while the executive order directs the Departments of Labor, Homeland Security, and State to scrutinize an H-1B employer's layoffs and contemplated layoffs throughout the H-1B process.

The 18 September proclamation extends the fee through 21 September 2027, mirroring the structure of the September 2025 proclamation that introduced this requirement. The executive order does not prohibit employers with layoffs from sponsoring H-1B workers; it directs agencies to consider layoffs as a factor.

The extension primarily affects foreign nationals outside the U.S. who require admission in H-1B status to begin employment under an approved H-1B petition, including individuals with newly approved H-1B petitions who have not yet entered the U.S., beneficiaries applying for an H-1B visa at a U.S. consulate abroad, and foreign nationals who must travel to the U.S. to activate an approved H-1B petition.

## Why it matters

The proclamation carries a critical caveat: the proclamation extends a policy that is currently vacated. Unless a court rules otherwise, USCIS remains barred from collecting it. Employers should not budget for the fee on the basis of the extension alone, while recognising that the posture could change quickly on appeal.

For the executive order's layoff requirement, until the agencies publish implementing rules or guidance, employers should expect questions about workforce reductions at the LCA, petition, visa, and admission stages. Congress deliberately confined non-displacement obligations to dependent employers and wilful violators in 1998, and applying a broader standard without a statute, rule making, or defined terms invites inconsistent adjudication and, in turn, litigation.

## Way forward

- **Monitor court docket on fee appeal.** The fee extension is not automatically enforceable because the underlying 2025 proclamation was vacated. Watch for appeals court decisions and ask your immigration counsel to track the case status in Massachusetts federal court.

- **Prepare for layoff inquiries at all stages.** Even without final guidance, anticipate questions about workforce reductions when filing labor condition applications, H-1B petitions, visa applications, and at port of entry. Document the timing, occupations, locations, and business reasons for any recent or planned layoffs.

- **Separate the two policies in client communications.** The executive order (layoff scrutiny) and proclamation (fee extension) operate independently. Brief clients that the fee remains legally blocked, while layoff considerations are already in play.

- **Flag previously filed cases.** The executive order applies to H-1B petitions that have already been filed, so audit pending cases for potential layoff activity at the sponsoring employer.
