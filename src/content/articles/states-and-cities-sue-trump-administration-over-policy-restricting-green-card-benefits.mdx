States, cities and counties filed lawsuits Monday challenging new federal rules that will allow immigrants' use of safety net resources to be taken into account for green cards and other applications to stay in or enter the country. The new federal policy goes into effect Friday and broadens the discretion of immigration officers reviewing applications for green cards, certain visas or admissions into the U.S., allowing officers to consider enrollment in public benefits, such as housing and food assistance, Medicaid and the Children's Health Insurance Program, as something called "public charge." If you represent clients in green card or visa cases—or if your clients rely on public assistance—this change requires immediate attention to your counseling and filing strategy.

## What changed

The policy allows immigration officers to consider enrollment in public benefits as "public charge," and when officers determine a person could become dependent on government benefits, that "public charge" is a ground for inadmissibility under federal law. Non-cash benefits were previously restricted from public charge consideration under the Biden administration. The Trump administration rescinded that 2022 policy, arguing that it is protecting taxpayer dollars and expects immigrants to be self-sufficient.

The scope of benefits now subject to public charge review includes food stamps (SNAP), housing vouchers, Medicaid, and the Children's Health Insurance Program (CHIP). DHS immigration officers reviewing I-485 applications, I-539 extensions of stay, consular processing interviews, and other admissibility determinations will now have discretion to weigh receipt of these benefits as evidence of likely future dependency, factoring that into inadmissibility findings.

## Why it matters

**Immediate impact on green card applicants:** The rule change, set to take effect Friday, would expand the list of possible government aid that immigration officials could cite as part of a reason to disqualify an applicant. If your client has received Medicaid, SNAP, housing assistance, or CHIP—or if a derivative or conditional resident family member has—DHS will now consider that use when assessing public charge risk. Even lawful permanent residents in derivative status can face complications if their household income falls and they enroll in benefits during adjustment proceedings.

**Mixed-status households and benefit "chilling effect":** A "climate of fear for immigrants" created by the Trump administration is already driving people away from signing up from government benefits and other assistance they qualify for – even if they're not subject to the new rule. U.S. citizen children and other eligible household members may forgo benefits to shield non-citizen family members from public charge risk, amplifying economic hardship.

**State and local fiscal consequences:** 22 states and the District of Columbia pointed to DHS estimates that say states nationwide may lose roughly $4.05 billion in annual federal transfer payments for Medicaid and CHIP programs alone. As immigrant families disenroll to protect immigration status, states and cities see reduced federal reimbursement and increased demand on emergency services.

**Practitioner trap:** You must now screen every I-485 applicant (and applicants for other status-granting visas) about prior or current receipt of public benefits. The burden shifts: advisors must now affirmatively counsel clients to avoid or limit benefit-claiming until after green card issuance or to pursue case-by-case waivers (Form I-601) if public charge inadmissibility is found.

## Way forward

- **Immediately audit your active caseload.** For any green card, I-539, or consular processing case pending or filed in the last three years, pull the I-485, I-864 (Affidavit of Support), and any benefit history. If the client or any household member received Medicaid, SNAP, housing assistance, or CHIP, assess public charge risk and consider whether amendment, waiver, or additional evidence (employment letters, assets, I-864 sponsor strength) is needed.

- **Revise intake and conflict-of-interest forms.** Add explicit screening questions: "Have you, your spouse, or your children received food stamps, Medicaid, housing vouchers, CHIP, or other government assistance in the past [5 years / 10 years]?" Document the answer and preserve it. Use this to flag public charge risk *before* filing and to advise clients that they should halt or postpone benefit enrollment until after green card issuance.

- **Counsel clients on timing and alternatives.** If a client is eligible for both green card adjustment and public benefits (e.g., pregnant and eligible for Medicaid), weigh whether to defer benefit enrollment until after I-485 approval. If circumstances demand immediate enrollment, preserve a paper trail showing medical necessity, job loss, or other hardship (not lifestyle choice) and gather strong compensatory evidence of self-sufficiency (employment, assets, education).

- **Prepare for increased denials and waivers.** As public charge determinations broaden, expect more I-485 denials and I-539 requests for waivers on public charge grounds (I-601). Build factual records now: employer letters, tax returns, bank statements, rental agreements, education credentials. Document reasons for any benefit use as temporary, medical, or extraordinary—not habitual dependency.
