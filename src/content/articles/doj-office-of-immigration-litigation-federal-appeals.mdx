The [Office of Immigration Litigation](https://www.justice.gov/civil/oil) is the component of the Department of Justice's [Civil Division](https://www.justice.gov/civil) that defends the federal government in nearly every immigration matter that reaches an Article III court. OIL is not part of [DOJ-EOIR](https://www.justice.gov/eoir), which houses the immigration courts and the Board of Immigration Appeals; it sits across the building, on the litigation side of Main Justice. When an asylum-seeker, an LPR fighting a [removal order under INA 237](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1227), or a denied family petitioner files a petition for review in a circuit court of appeals, OIL is the lawyer arguing the other side. Its [Appellate Section](https://www.justice.gov/civil/office-immigration-litigation-appellate-section) handles the petition-for-review docket; its [District Court Section](https://www.justice.gov/civil/office-immigration-litigation-district-court-section) handles habeas, mandamus, and [Administrative Procedure Act](https://www.justice.gov/jmd/file/831166/download) challenges to immigration regulations.

## What changed

OIL was [established in 1979](https://www.justice.gov/sites/default/files/civil/legacy/2011/02/02/CivilDivisionHistory.pdf) as a specialist unit within the Civil Division to consolidate immigration appellate defense across all twelve regional circuits and the [D.C. Circuit](https://www.cadc.uscourts.gov). Its docket has scaled with the underlying flow of [BIA decisions](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/board-of-immigration-appeals); OIL now handles [over 7,000 petitions for review per year](https://www.justice.gov/civil/oil) and a parallel district-court docket of [several thousand habeas, mandamus, and APA cases](https://www.justice.gov/civil/office-immigration-litigation-district-court-section). The [Appellate Section](https://www.justice.gov/civil/office-immigration-litigation-appellate-section) is organized into seven regional teams that map roughly to the circuits, each staffed with attorneys who specialize in the jurisprudence and procedural quirks of their assigned courts.

The petition-for-review jurisdictional architecture sits at [INA 242](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1252), which channels review of final orders of removal exclusively to the courts of appeals and imposes a [30-day filing deadline](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1252) from the date the BIA issues a final order. Section 242(a)(2) carves out several jurisdiction-stripping provisions — discretionary denials, criminal-alien bars at [INA 242(a)(2)(C)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1252), the [REAL ID Act's question-of-law/constitutional-claim preservation](https://www.congress.gov/bill/109th-congress/house-bill/418/text) at INA 242(a)(2)(D), and a series of bars layered on through the [IIRIRA reforms](https://www.congress.gov/bill/104th-congress/house-bill/3610) of 1996. OIL's appellate posture is built around enforcing those bars where it can and arguing the substantive merits where it cannot.

OIL also represents the government in [Supreme Court immigration cases](https://www.supremecourt.gov/), typically in coordination with the [Office of the Solicitor General](https://www.justice.gov/osg). Recent merits cases that shaped the doctrine — [Niz-Chavez v. Garland](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/20pdf/19-863_6jfm.pdf) on what counts as a notice to appear for stop-time purposes, [Wilkinson v. Garland](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/22-666_d18e.pdf) on jurisdictional review of exceptional-and-extremely-unusual-hardship determinations, [Patel v. Garland](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/21pdf/20-979_h315.pdf) on review of factual findings underlying discretionary denials — moved through OIL at the certiorari and merits-briefing stages.

OIL leadership and posture shift with each administration. [The current Director](https://www.justice.gov/civil/office-immigration-litigation) is appointed without Senate confirmation and operates under guidance from the [Assistant Attorney General for the Civil Division](https://www.justice.gov/civil/staff-profile/meet-civil-division-leadership) and ultimately the [Deputy Attorney General](https://www.justice.gov/dag). Acquiescence and nonacquiescence decisions — whether to apply an adverse circuit ruling nationwide or limit it to the issuing circuit — flow from those leadership channels and shape the next petitioner's likelihood of success.

## Why it matters

For a removal-defense practitioner filing a [petition for review](https://www.uscourts.gov/forms/notice-appeal-and-statements-jurisdiction/petition-review-agency-order), the opposing party on every brief is OIL. The realistic odds of relief depend heavily on which circuit you are in and which OIL regional team you are facing. The Ninth Circuit, which receives the highest volume of immigration PFRs, has a body of pro-petitioner [particular-social-group case law](https://www.ca9.uscourts.gov/) and a long line of [credibility-determination remands](https://www.ca9.uscourts.gov/) that OIL's San Francisco-based attorneys are familiar with and litigate aggressively. The Fifth Circuit has [substantially more deferential precedent](https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/) on credibility and on the [Circumvention rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/05/16/2023-10146/circumvention-of-lawful-pathways). The Second Circuit's [particular-social-group jurisprudence](https://www.ca2.uscourts.gov/) and its [REAL ID-era treatment of inconsistencies](https://www.ca2.uscourts.gov/) draws OIL into substantive debates about credibility-finding standards that other circuits have already settled.

OIL's nonacquiescence posture also reshapes the value of a favorable circuit decision. A favorable Ninth Circuit ruling on, say, the [stop-time rule under Niz-Chavez](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/20pdf/19-863_6jfm.pdf) before the Supreme Court resolved the question was binding in the Ninth but the BIA — represented by OIL on appeal — continued to apply its contrary reading in the other eleven circuits until the Supreme Court spoke. A practitioner relying on cross-circuit precedent without confirming the BIA's nationwide application posture, which is reflected in [OIL's briefing](https://www.justice.gov/civil/oil), can be surprised at the immigration-court stage.

For district-court litigation, OIL's [District Court Section](https://www.justice.gov/civil/office-immigration-litigation-district-court-section) is the office defending agency regulations against [APA challenges](https://www.justice.gov/jmd/file/831166/download) — including the [Circumvention of Lawful Pathways rule](https://www.aclu.org/cases/east-bay-sanctuary-covenant-v-biden), the [public-charge final rules](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/09/09/2022-18867/public-charge-ground-of-inadmissibility), the [DACA rescission](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/19pdf/18-587_5ifl.pdf) and reissuance litigation, and the [parole policies underlying CBP One processing](https://www.dhs.gov/news/2023/01/05/dhs-continues-prepare-end-title-42-announces-new-border-enforcement-measures-and). Counsel filing an APA challenge needs to anticipate OIL's standing, ripeness, and APA-section-704 finality arguments, which the office has built into a [recurring playbook](https://www.justice.gov/civil/office-immigration-litigation-district-court-section).

## Way forward

If you are filing a petition for review, calendar the [30-day filing window](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1252) from the date of the BIA final order, not the date your client received the decision; the deadline is jurisdictional under [Stone v. INS, 514 U.S. 386 (1995)](https://www.supremecourt.gov/cases/federal/us/514/386/) and OIL routinely moves to dismiss late filings. File in the [circuit where the immigration judge completed the proceedings](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1252) and consider whether a [stay of removal motion](https://www.uscourts.gov/about-federal-courts/federal-courts-public/court-website-links) is needed under [Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418 (2009)](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/08pdf/08-681.pdf) — OIL opposes most stay motions and the Nken four-factor analysis is where many cases are won or lost before merits briefing.

Read OIL's brief carefully when it arrives. The office's regional specialists have a strong sense of which arguments their assigned circuit responds to; an OIL brief that leads with a [jurisdictional argument under INA 242(a)(2)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1252) is signaling that the merits position is weaker than the bar argument, and the petitioner's reply should engage both.

For district-court APA challenges, anticipate OIL's standard playbook on standing, finality, [APA Section 706 arbitrary-and-capricious framing](https://www.justice.gov/jmd/file/831166/download), and the [Chevron-now-Loper-Bright](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/22-451_7m58.pdf) deference debate. The arrival of Loper Bright in [June 2024](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/22-451_7m58.pdf) shifted OIL's defense of agency regulations meaningfully and counsel should plan briefing around the post-Chevron framework rather than the legacy doctrine.

If your client is in active removal proceedings and you are evaluating a long-shot appellate posture, weigh OIL's likely treatment of the issue across circuits — the [office publishes guidance and policy memoranda](https://www.justice.gov/civil/oil) intermittently but its real posture shows in the briefs filed across the regional teams.

## Disclaimer

[Fola Editorial](https://www.justice.gov/civil/oil) publishes general information here, not legal advice. Federal appellate practice in immigration matters is technical and circuit-dependent. For a live PFR or district-court matter, retain immigration appellate counsel and verify the current rule and precedent directly at [justice.gov](https://www.justice.gov), the relevant circuit court's website, and [uscourts.gov](https://www.uscourts.gov).
