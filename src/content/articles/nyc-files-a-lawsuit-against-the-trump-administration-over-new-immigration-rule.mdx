New York Attorney General Letitia James and a coalition of 21 other states and the District of Columbia filed suit Monday to block a new Department of Homeland Security "public charge" rule. NYC Mayor Zohran Mamdani separately led a coalition of cities in a parallel lawsuit challenging the same rule. The rule is set to take effect Friday, September 18, 2026. The lawsuits create an immediate litigation risk for the policy and signal major pushback from Democratic-led jurisdictions on who can get permanent residency.

## What changed

The 2022 Biden rule limited public charge determinations to cash assistance for income maintenance or long-term institutionalization at government expense. The Trump administration's new rule, taking effect September 18, would let immigration officers count nearly any public benefit, used for any length of time, against an applicant.

The final rule issued in July reverses the 2022 Biden-era policy that only allowed authorities to consider a green-card applicant's use of cash benefits. The new version provides no limits. Any use of a public benefit, as well as applicant metrics such as age, education, skills and health, can be considered for legal permanent residence as well as for immigrants seeking to enter the country.

Under the revised standard, immigration officers may now weigh applicants' use of the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP, commonly called food stamps), Medicaid, the Children's Health Insurance Program (CHIP), housing vouchers, and the Women, Infants, and Children (WIC) nutrition program when deciding whether to grant a green card. The Trump administration's 2026 rule dismantles a 27-year-old 1999 framework that clarified that Medicaid, food stamps, WIC, housing assistance, and child care subsidies did not count as public charge.

The rule also includes benefits legally used by family members, even if the family member is a U.S. citizen. Under the rule, a noncitizen parent's green card application could be at risk because their U.S. citizen child received benefits.

Anyone who files their green card application with USCIS before September 18, 2026 (or already has a pending application that was filed before this date) will be decided based on the 2022 policy.

## Why it matters

The rule change creates an immediate chilling effect on benefit-seeking by immigrant families and reshapes green card application strategy for practitioners.

USCIS itself acknowledges its approach will cause 1.3 million people to leave Medicaid, the Children's Health Insurance Program (CHIP), SNAP, TANF, SSI, federal rental assistance, and the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children (WIC). Many of these disenrollments will occur before any green card application is even adjudicated.

The litigation brought by the states and cities asks the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York to block the unlawful measure so that residents do not have to fear that seeking help today could jeopardize their – or their family members' – immigration status in the future. This means the rule's implementation may be halted or delayed by preliminary injunction before September 18, though that is not certain.

For practitioners, the implications include:

- **Filing deadlines matter.** The filing date decides which rule applies to a case. File before September 18, 2026, and the case is judged under the current rule. File on or after that date, and the new rule applies instead.

- **Form updates.** USCIS is releasing a new version of Form I-485, the form used to apply for a green card from inside the United States. Starting September 18, the old version of the form will no longer be accepted.

- **Family benefit use now counts.** Practitioners must warn clients that even a U.S. citizen child's receipt of Medicaid or SNAP could theoretically be cited in adjudication of the parent's green card application under the new discretionary standard.

## Way forward

As a practitioner or applicant, take these steps now:

- **Assess filing urgency.** If your client has a green card application ready, filing before September 18 locks in the more favorable 2022 standard. This is a hard deadline with material consequences.

- **Monitor litigation.** The coalition is asking the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York to block this unlawful measure. Check the [Southern District of New York docket](https://www.nysd.uscourts.gov/) for emergency motions and preliminary injunction decisions, which could pause or vacate the rule before it takes effect.

- **Counsel on benefits.** Clients with pending green card applications filed on or after September 18 should expect immigration officers to have broad discretion to consider any public benefit use. Advise clients to document self-sufficiency, employment history, and family support in preparation.

- **Review family structure.** For applicants with U.S. citizen family members, discuss whether those family members' benefit use could create vulnerabilities under the new discretionary standard, and whether the applicant should pursue alternative visa categories that may have fewer public charge scrutiny.
