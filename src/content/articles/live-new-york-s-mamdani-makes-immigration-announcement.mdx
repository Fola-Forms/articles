New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani announced that New York City, alongside the City of Chicago, the City and County of San Francisco, the County of Santa Clara, the City of Seattle, King County, Washington and the Public Rights Project, filed a lawsuit challenging the federal government's new public charge rule. The lawsuit, filed in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York, comes alongside a separate legal challenge led by New York State Attorney General Letitia James. Immigration practitioners must understand the rule change and the judicial challenge now underway.

## What changed

The Trump administration's new rule, taking effect September 18, would let immigration officers count nearly any public benefit, used for any length of time, against an applicant. This is a significant expansion from prior guidance. In 2022, the federal government issued a rule limiting public charge determinations to cash assistance for income maintenance or long-term institutionalization at government expense.

In August, USCIS issued guidance to provide more information on how immigration officers will determine if immigrants applying for green cards are likely to be a "public charge," or an immigrant determined to depend primarily on the government to live. The August 2026 guidance instructs immigration officers to follow the statute by considering all relevant information, including age, health, family status, financial status, and education, and applying a "totality of the circumstances" analysis.

The practical effect: A new federal rule taking effect Friday lets immigration officers weigh Medicaid, SNAP and housing aid when deciding who can get a green card or visa.

Two separate lawsuits now challenge the rule:

- New York City is leading a coalition that includes Chicago, San Francisco, Seattle, Santa Clara County, King County, Washington, and the nonprofit Public Rights Project. Both were filed Monday in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York.
- Attorney General Letitia James is leading a separate suit joined by 21 other states and the District of Columbia.

## Why it matters

The expanded rule creates a chilling effect on benefit use for immigrant families—regardless of whether they will ultimately be subject to the public charge test.

Attorney General James and the coalition assert that the administration has acknowledged, and even celebrated, that the fear and confusion the new rule would create could cause immigrant families, including U.S. citizen children, to disenroll from benefits to which they are legally entitled. The federal government also acknowledged that a previous iteration of a similar rule caused benefit disenrollment rates as high as 35 percent among mixed-status families, and as high as 60 percent among refugees.

For practitioners, this means:

- **Client counseling shifts.** You may need to explicitly advise clients who are applying for green cards, renewal of visas, or consular processing about the new public charge standard and the risks of benefit use during the application period.
- **Mixed-status families are at highest risk.** The states' lawsuit argues they would lose billions of dollars in federal funding if immigrants, particularly mixed-status families, disenroll from programs due to fears about immigration consequences.
- **The rule affects multiple benefit types.** Income-based Medicaid, SNAP, housing assistance, and other means-tested benefits are now on the table for consideration by USCIS officers.
- **The injunction outcome is uncertain.** It is unclear whether the lawsuits will stop the rule change.

## Way forward

- **Read the August 2026 USCIS guidance yourself.** Visit the [USCIS website](https://www.uscis.gov) and locate the public charge guidance finalized this summer. Understand the "totality of circumstances" factors the agency says it will apply.

- **Communicate the rule change to clients applying for green cards (I-485) or visa renewals.** Do not assume they already know. Explain that benefit use—even lawful, authorized use—may now be considered by USCIS when adjudicating their application. This is particularly urgent for cases with an anticipated filing in the next 30–60 days.

- **Track the litigation.** The lawsuits were filed September 14, 2026, in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York. Check for a preliminary injunction motion and order, which could pause the rule before the September 18 start date. Decisions may come quickly.

- **Document your client's circumstances now.** If a client is receiving means-tested benefits and expects to file an I-485 or I-539 (extension of stay) soon, consider evidence of financial hardship, family ties, employment, and other "totality" factors independent of benefit use. The more you can show a client's resilience and ties to the U.S., the less the benefit use will sting.
