A three-judge panel of the 1st US Circuit Court of Appeals in Boston largely upheld a February decision that found the Department of Homeland Security's so-called third-country removal policy unlawful. The ruling imposes strict due-process requirements on DHS before it can deport migrants to countries other than their home nation, and is likely to reshape removal adjudication in cases involving third-country placements.

## What changed

The 1st Circuit largely upheld a February district-court decision striking down DHS's third-country removal policy as unlawful. US Circuit Judge Seth Aframe held that migrants must be given a "meaningful" chance to raise safety concerns before removal, rejecting the Trump administration's narrower reading of the law.

The court did overturn part of the lower-court ruling on procedural grounds, concerning whether officials must first try deporting migrants to countries they have ties to. The net effect: DHS cannot use its streamlined third-country removal pathway without first satisfying statutory due-process obligations.

The case stems from a class-action lawsuit testing what due process migrants are owed before being sent to nations with which they have no ties. The policy, adopted in March 2025, allowed removals with only minimal notice if officials had diplomatic assurances against persecution. The court found this insufficient.

## Why it matters

For practitioners, this ruling reshapes the timeline and burden in any third-country removal case. Under Trump, the US has struck agreements allowing it to send more than 25,000 migrants to at least 29 third countries, most often Mexico. Until now, DHS could move quickly; the 1st Circuit's decision imposes a meaningful-notice and safety-interview step that did not exist under the March 2025 policy.

Clients facing removal to a third country now have a **right** to a hearing on whether they fear persecution or torture in that destination. This is not a new substantive protection (it existed in statute); rather, the court held that DHS cannot bypass it via summary process or diplomatic assurances alone.

The ruling is also significant because the policy has reached the Supreme Court twice, and in 2025 the justices cleared the way for eight men, including nationals of Cuba, Myanmar and Vietnam, to be deported to South Sudan, a country the US State Department advises against visiting because of "crime, kidnapping, and armed conflict". The appellate panel's decision may prompt further Supreme Court review, but for now, it stands as binding law in the 1st Circuit (Maine, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Puerto Rico, and Rhode Island) and as persuasive authority elsewhere.

## Way forward

- **If your client is in removal proceedings in the 1st Circuit and faces a third-country placement**, immediately file or preserve a motion for a hearing at which your client can present evidence of fear of persecution or torture in the proposed third country.

- **Document the client's ties (or lack thereof) to the third country.** The court emphasized that due process requires consideration of whether the client has any connection to the destination nation.

- **Obtain country-conditions evidence** on the third-country destination. The court's opinion signals skepticism of DHS reliance on "diplomatic assurances" alone; documented State Department warnings, human-rights reports, and witness testimony may be crucial.

- **Monitor for Supreme Court cert.** This case will likely return to SCOTUS. Subscribe to SCOTUS blog or consult appellate counsel to track petitions and filings.
