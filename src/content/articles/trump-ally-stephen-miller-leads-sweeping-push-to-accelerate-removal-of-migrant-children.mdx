Stephen Miller, White House deputy chief of staff for policy, is directing a coordinated, multi-agency effort to accelerate removal of undocumented immigrant children from the United States. The campaign involves HHS, DHS, DOJ, and other departments and is already affecting legal representation, detention conditions, and deportation timelines for minors in federal custody.

## What changed

Miller is leading an unprecedented and intense White House drive across a host of government departments to accelerate the removal of undocumented immigrant children from the US. As deputy chief of staff at the White House, focused on immigration policy, Miller is seen as the mastermind and enforcer behind Donald Trump's hardline anti-immigration agenda, directing a whole-of-government approach that has been ramping up throughout Trump's second term and is targeting a vulnerable population of undocumented children.

The departments include Health and Human Services (HHS), which oversees the care of migrant children who are in the country without a parent or guardian; the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), where Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) and US Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) are based; and the Department of Justice (DoJ), which houses the immigration courts. Miller frequently confers with subordinates, daily at times, pressing them to maximize removals of immigrant children from the US, according to a number of sources familiar with the White House plans.

Two significant operational changes are already in effect:

**Legal services contract lapse.** HHS refused to renew the contract for the legal services to represent these children after stiffing lawyers seven months of pay to the tune of $65 million. With it, the legal lifeline for an estimated 25,000 to 26,000 children was severed.

**New contractor arrangement.** A $244 million contract went to Our Rescue, an NGO whose founder was accused of sex trafficking and abusing women and whose CEO had a tenure as a DHS agent lowlighted by setting up a fake university to entrap and then arrest foreign students.

## Why it matters

The loss of legal representation has immediate consequences in immigration proceedings. The Guardian reported from immigration court in New York City, "the faces of migrant children — some as young as three — have been beamed onto a video screen throughout the summer as they faced deportation hearings." These children did not have lawyers, in violation of aforementioned federal law, and thus faced interrogation by Trump appointee Judge Lisa Ling.

This is a scene taking place out of public view across the country, impacting 24,000 kids. Legal experts cited in the reporting say children are being held an average of 194 days, far beyond the 20-day limit set by law.

For practitioners representing unaccompanied minors:
- **Representation availability**: The contract lapse created a gap in legal services during a critical period. Even though nonprofits have since recovered back pay, the disruption has already affected case outcomes.
- **Procedural due process**: Children appearing in removal hearings without counsel—a federal requirement for unaccompanied minors in immigration court—may create grounds for appeal or reopening of cases.
- **Facility and contractor vetting**: The shift to a new contractor raises questions about how minors' interests are prioritized in case management and care coordination.

## Way forward

**1. Verify representation status immediately.** If you represent an unaccompanied minor or advocate for one, confirm whether they have been assigned counsel or have access to representation. The representation gap affected cases from August through September 2026.

**2. Screen for hearing without counsel.** If a client appeared at a removal hearing without an attorney present, document this fact. It may support a motion to reopen the removal proceeding under INA §240(c).

**3. Monitor contractor transitions.** Track which organization is now providing care and legal coordination for your clients. Changes in contractor can affect case file access, communication pathways, and facility conditions.

**4. Document conditions.** If clients are held beyond the 20-day statutory preference period, note dates and circumstances for potential litigation or relief applications.
