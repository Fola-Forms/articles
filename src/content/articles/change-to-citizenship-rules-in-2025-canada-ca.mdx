Bill C-3, An Act to Amend the Citizenship Act (2025), was introduced on June 5, 2025, and came into effect on December 15, 2025. The legislation eliminates a long-standing restriction on how Canadian parents born or adopted abroad can pass citizenship to their children. This change opens pathways to Canadian citizenship for thousands of second-generation and later individuals who were previously ineligible.

## What changed

Before Bill C-3, Canadian citizenship by descent was limited to the first generation born outside Canada—a rule called the first-generation limit to citizenship. A Canadian citizen could only pass on citizenship to a child born or adopted outside Canada if the parent was either born or naturalized in Canada before the child's birth or adoption.

Bill C-3 removed this limit in some situations. People who automatically became Canadian citizens under the new law can apply to get proof of Canadian citizenship. Additionally, people adopted abroad before December 15, 2025, by a Canadian parent born or adopted abroad can apply for Canadian citizenship for an adopted child.

The first generation is the first person born or adopted outside Canada to a Canadian citizen who was born in Canada or granted citizenship (naturalized). Individuals born or adopted after that first generation—second generation or later—may now qualify for automatic recognition as Canadian citizens if their parent is a Canadian citizen, regardless of whether that parent was born in Canada or abroad.

## Why it matters

For practitioners advising clients on citizenship eligibility, this represents a fundamental shift in Canadian law. Individuals who were previously told they couldn't claim Canadian citizenship through a parent who was also born abroad—the "lost Canadians" category—may now qualify automatically.

People born before December 15, 2025, who would have been citizens but for the first-generation limit or other outdated rules, will be considered Canadian and can now apply for proof of Canadian citizenship. This retroactive eligibility creates new filing opportunities for clients who may have been rejected or discouraged from applying in the past.

This change to the first-generation limit doesn't affect anyone who already became a Canadian citizen before Bill C-3 became law—if you were already a citizen before the bill, you're still a citizen. The change is prospective for children born on or after the effective date, but retroactively restores eligibility for those born before it.

Note that if this change made someone a Canadian automatically, but they don't want to be one, they can apply to give up (renounce) their Canadian citizenship.

## Way forward

- **Check eligibility immediately.** Review your client roster for second-generation individuals born abroad to Canadian parents who were also born or adopted outside Canada. These individuals may now have automatic citizenship rights they did not before.

- **Gather documentation.** Prepare birth certificates, Canadian citizenship documents for the parent, and proof of the parent's Canadian status at the time of your client's birth or adoption.

- **File for proof of citizenship.** IRCC now recognizes new eligible citizens and is applying the new rules for passing citizenship on going forward. Submit an application for a Certificate of Canadian Citizenship through IRCC's official portal.

- **Monitor processing times.** Given the volume of retroactive claims expected to flow from this change, verify current processing timeframes before advising clients on wait times.
