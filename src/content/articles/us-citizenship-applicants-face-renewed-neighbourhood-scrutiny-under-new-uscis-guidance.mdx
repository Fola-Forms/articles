USCIS has officially ended a three-decade waiver of neighborhood investigations for citizenship applicants, resuming a statutory practice dormant since 1991. The policy memo, dated Aug. 22 and released publicly Tuesday, directs USCIS to resume personal investigations of aliens applying for naturalization under section 335(a) of the Immigration and Nationality Act. This shifts the naturalization vetting landscape and requires practitioners to advise clients on new pre-filing preparation steps.

## What changed

The USCIS memo immediately terminated the "general waiver" for neighborhood checks, directing officers to determine whether such investigations are warranted based on the information, or lack thereof, submitted by citizenship applicants. The personal investigations will include interviewing a naturalization applicant's neighbors and employment history stretching back at least five years before an individual applied for naturalization.

USCIS officers will decide whether to carry out a neighborhood investigation by requesting and reviewing testimonial letters from neighbors, employers, coworkers and business associates who know the person applying for U.S. citizenship. The checks are to ensure "scrutiny of an alien's residency, good moral character, attachment to the U.S. Constitution, and disposition to the good order and happiness of the United States."

Officers retain the ability to waive the checks, according to the memo.

## Why it matters

This policy reversal fundamentally changes client preparation for N-400 naturalization filings. For three decades, applicants relied primarily on FBI criminal and security background checks; neighborhood investigations were available but essentially dormant. Now, USCIS expects officers to use them as a default investigative tool rather than an exception.

The memo suggested that citizenship applicants should "proactively" submit testimonial letters, to avoid receiving requests for more evidence. The agency said failure or refusal to comply with a request for evidence could lead to a neighborhood investigation and "impact" applicants' ability to show they qualify for U.S. citizenship. This creates a strategic incentive: clients who preemptively package letters of recommendation from neighbors, employers, and community members may reduce friction in adjudication.

The five-year employment lookback and resident scrutiny mean clients with stable, documentable community roots and employment records will move faster. Those with job changes, relocations, or thin community ties should expect higher USCIS scrutiny.

## Way forward

- **Gather testimonials early.** Before filing, have your client solicit letters from at least 5–10 neighbors, employers, supervisors, and community members attesting to good moral character, lawful behavior, and attachment to U.S. values. Include these proactively with the N-400 packet.

- **Document employment for five years back.** Assemble pay stubs, W-2s, offer letters, and employer certifications covering the full lookback period. If your client changed jobs, obtain reference letters from prior employers confirming stable, lawful employment.

- **Verify address history.** Gather utility bills, lease agreements, mortgage documents, and voter registration records showing residence in the U.S. for the required period (3 or 5 years). USCIS may contact neighbors; a clear paper trail helps.

- **Address red flags proactively.** If your client has prior arrests (even without conviction), traffic citations, financial issues, or gaps in employment history, prepare narrative statements and supporting evidence explaining them. Anticipate the neighborhood investigation and frame the record affirmatively.
