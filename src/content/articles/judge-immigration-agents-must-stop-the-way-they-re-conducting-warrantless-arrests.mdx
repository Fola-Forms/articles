U.S. District Judge Maame Ewusi-Mensah Frimpong has finalized a ruling that immigration agents cannot arrest someone in the country unlawfully without first determining if they're likely to flee before a warrant can be obtained. The order applies to Los Angeles and surrounding areas and signals a fundamental shift in how immigration officers may conduct arrests in the region.

## What changed

The judge found "ample evidence that Defendants have an ongoing policy and practice of conducting warrantless arrests in violation of the requirement to conduct a likelihood-of-escape analysis." Under federal law, immigration officers can arrest someone without a warrant only when they have probable cause both that the person is in the country unlawfully and that they are likely to escape before a warrant can be obtained.

The order requires immigration agents to perform an individualized, documented assessment of flight risk *before* making a warrantless arrest. Advocates argued the analysis should include community ties, like whether a person has a job and a home and a family, but the judge declined to require those specifics if the escape risk can be shown through other factors.

The judge also declined to require agents to document cases where they talk to someone but don't arrest them. The government asked for a two-week delay to decide whether to appeal before the order takes effect, but Frimpong denied that, saying the government "cannot reasonably assert that it is harmed in any legally cognizable sense" by being ordered to follow the law.

## Why it matters

This finalized order binds immigration agents in the Central District of California and serves as persuasive authority in other jurisdictions. If you represent clients in removal proceedings or advise individuals facing immigration enforcement in this region, the ruling changes the basis on which ICE can conduct arrests without a warrant.

Practically, agents can no longer rely on a bare assertion that someone is deportable. They must document specific facts—behaviors, statements, or circumstances—showing flight risk *before* custody begins. This creates opportunities to challenge the lawfulness of arrests and potentially vacate findings that clients refused to leave or sign documents during unlawful detention.

The order does *not* require agents to consider community ties (employment, family, property ownership) as probative of *non*-flight risk. Advocacy organizations may still argue that such ties are relevant to show likelihood that someone will appear for proceedings, but the judge declined to mandate this, meaning agents may satisfy the order's requirement through other means.

## Way forward

- **Document every detail of arrest circumstances**: If you represent someone arrested without a warrant by immigration agents in Los Angeles or surrounding areas, immediately obtain body camera footage, arrest reports, and agent declarations. Check whether the narrative states specific facts about flight risk *before* custody began, or whether it is conclusory.
  
- **Challenge unlawful arrests in removal proceedings**: If an agent arrested your client without a warrant and the record shows no documented pre-arrest flight-risk analysis, argue the arrest violated the Fourth Amendment and this binding court order. File a motion to suppress any statements or evidence obtained as fruit of the unlawful arrest.

- **Preserve appellate record**: Even if an immigration judge denies your suppression motion, note for the record that the arrest violated *Vasquez Perdomo v. Mullin* and the Central District's binding order. This supports appellate review and may support an application for legal permanent residence if grounds exist.

- **Advise clients of rights**: If a client is approached by immigration agents, remind them they may decline consent to search, refuse to sign documents, and demand to speak to a lawyer. Anything said or done *after* an unlawful arrest may be excludable in removal proceedings under this order.
