The Trump administration has implemented a significant expansion of the "public charge" rule that will reshape how immigration officers evaluate green card applications. Under the new rule, officials reviewing green card applications are able to consider a broader scope of public benefit programs. The rule, which goes into effect on Friday, updates a policy dating back to the 19th century that the U.S. has long used to prohibit immigrants who are believed to be likely to become a "public charge" from getting green cards.

## What changed

Historically, officials reviewing green card applications have only taken into account cash benefit programs—for instance, Supplemental Security Income from Social Security—as "public benefits" that could lead to an immigrant being deemed a "public charge." They have usually not deemed non-cash public benefits, such as Medicaid and food stamps, as programs that would disqualify an immigrant from obtaining permanent resident status.

The new rule fundamentally reverses this approach. The final rule, published Monday, allows immigration officers to take into account a wider array of public assistance programs when determining whether green card applicants will likely become "public charges," which is one factor in green card adjudication.

Trump's Department of Homeland Security (DHS) has argued that the change "is restoring the basic principle that immigrants must be able to support themselves," adding that it is "reaffirming the requirement of self-reliance, protecting public resources, and ending policies that encouraged dependency on hard-working American taxpayers."

The new rule is similar to what was implemented by the first Trump administration but more expansive.

## Why it matters

This expansion has two immediate consequences for practitioners and applicants:

**Green card adjudication standards shift.** Any applicant who has used or is deemed likely to need food stamps, Medicaid, housing vouchers, or other non-cash assistance can now be rejected on "public charge" grounds. This creates a new and material risk factor in family-based immigration cases and adjustment-of-status filings.

**Chilling effect on benefit enrollment.** The rule could also have a "chilling effect" on hundreds of thousands of immigrant families, prompting them to disenroll from or not to apply for safety net programs that they – and particularly their US citizen children – are eligible to receive for fear of hurting their chances to be approved. Some 3.7 million members of immigrant households could lose Medicaid, food stamps, housing benefits and other assistance, according to a George Washington University study. State economies could lose $27.4 billion as healthcare providers, grocery stores and other businesses lose revenue, and up to 212,000 jobs could be lost.

Immigration practitioners will need to counsel green card applicants—especially those with family members on benefits—about the heightened risk of denial, and may face difficult advising situations where applicants must choose between accessing safety-net programs and pursuing permanent resident status.

## Way forward

- **Audit client benefit history immediately.** If you represent green card applicants or adjustment-of-status filers, review their household's use of cash and non-cash public benefits. This information will now be part of the adjudication record.

- **Consider timing and strategy.** In some cases, applicants may need to wait for household benefit status to change, or restructure household composition, before filing. Consult on a case-by-case basis.

- **Monitor litigation.** A new Trump administration rule that is expected to make it harder for many legal immigrants to obtain green cards if they receive or are deemed likely to need public assistance went into effect Friday – even as lawsuits seeking to halt it wind their way through federal court. Court injunctions or reversals could change the landscape.

- **Flag for family members.** If a green card applicant is considering applying, advise household members already in the U.S. about the risks of applying for federal benefits while an application is pending.
