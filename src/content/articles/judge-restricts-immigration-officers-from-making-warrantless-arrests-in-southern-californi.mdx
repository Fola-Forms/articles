A federal judge has barred immigration enforcement officers from making arrests without a warrant in Southern California. U.S. District Judge Maame Ewusi-Mensah Frimpong granted a preliminary injunction on Sept. 2, which was unsealed on Wednesday. If you represent clients in the Central District of California or advise on Fourth Amendment challenges to immigration arrests, this ruling materially affects your litigation and client-counseling strategy.

## What changed

The American Civil Liberties Union of Southern California, Public Counsel, and Los Angeles immigrant advocacy groups filed a lawsuit against the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) last year after the region saw a massive surge in immigration enforcement. The preliminary injunction was granted on Sept. 2 and covers the Central District of California, which includes Los Angeles, Orange, San Bernardino, Ventura and San Luis Obispo counties.

Judge Frimpong ordered the federal government to thoroughly document the probable cause that leads to an arrest before obtaining a warrant, in order to prove the arrests were "proper." This creates a heightened documentary burden on ICE/CBP in the district: they cannot simply rely on administrative findings or form-filling; they must affirmatively establish probable cause in writing *before* seeking a warrant.

## Why it matters

This preliminary injunction binds ICE and CBP operations in five Southern California counties. For practitioners:

- **Habeas corpus and suppression motions**: You now have judicial precedent requiring the government to produce contemporaneous documentation of probable cause before a warrant was sought. Absent such documentation, you have stronger grounds to challenge the lawfulness of the arrest and the validity of the detention.
- **Discovery leverage**: When opposing a removal case in that district, you can demand production of the probable-cause analysis conducted *before* the warrant was obtained, not post-hoc justifications.
- **Client counseling**: Clients arrested in these counties can be advised that officers were under a court order requiring pre-warrant documentation—a fact relevant to Fourth Amendment arguments and credibility.
- **Persuasive weight**: Judges in Oregon, Colorado, Washington, D.C., and the Eastern District of California have issued similar orders in the past year, meaning this is part of a broader judicial trend restricting warrantless immigration arrests nationwide.

## Way forward

- **If you are handling a removal defense case in the Central District of California**: File a supplemental brief citing Judge Frimpong's preliminary injunction when arguing Fourth Amendment violations or challenging the lawfulness of arrest. Request production of the government's pre-warrant probable-cause documentation under this order.
- **If your client was arrested in these five counties**: Obtain and review the arrest report and any warrant application to see whether the government actually documented probable cause *before* seeking the warrant, as required by this order. File a suppression motion if documentation is missing or conclusory.
- **If you work in removal defense nationally**: Monitor this case on appeal and use it as persuasive authority in other districts. Similar challenges may succeed elsewhere.
- **Stay informed on updates**: Preliminary injunctions can be stayed or reversed on appeal. Check the Central District of California docket regularly for any appeals or modifications.
