A federal appeals court handed the Trump administration a key win this week in its efforts to hold illegal immigrants in detention pending their removal proceedings, as a key part of President Donald Trump's aggressive immigration agenda makes its way closer to the Supreme Court. On July 21–22, the full U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit issued an en banc stay order that will reshape detention practice in removal cases across the circuit.

## What changed

The full bench of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit halted lower court orders that had released a trio of illegal immigrants from detention and ordered they be subject to bond hearings if federal authorities wanted to attempt to detain them again. The stay applies to consolidated cases including *Ignacio Sosnava Rodriguez v. Sylvester M. Ortega* and related cases.

The order from the full Fifth Circuit did not elaborate on its decision to halt the district court orders, but it came weeks after the circuit three-judge panel's ruling was set aside so the full court could hear the case. Both the district courts and the three-judge panel had ruled that the indefinite detention of the three illegal immigrants had violated their due process rights under the Fifth Amendment.

The underlying panel decision (July 2, 2026) had held that noncitizens who entered without inspection but have established long-term U.S. residence are entitled to individualized bond hearings after 90 days of continuous detention. The stay order now permits the government to continue holding them without such hearings while the full circuit considers the case on the merits.

## Why it matters

This is a critical junction point for removal-proceedings detention law. The Fifth Circuit, along with the Eighth Circuit, found that the administration has the power to detain illegal immigrants indefinitely pending removal proceedings under the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996. But the question of whether the due process clause of the Fifth Amendment requires bond hearings and release will be at the center of arguments at the Fifth Circuit.

For now, the stay order freezes the lower courts' pro-release rulings. This means:

- **Immediate impact**: Any noncitizen currently detained in the Fifth Circuit (Texas, Louisiana, Mississippi) cannot rely on district court orders granting bond hearings or release; the stay applies regardless of individual circumstances.
- **Appellate momentum**: The full en banc decision signals that a majority of Fifth Circuit judges are likely to reverse the panel's due-process holding and affirm the administration's mandatory detention authority.
- **Supreme Court trajectory**: This case will almost certainly reach SCOTUS. The Fifth Circuit's en banc opinion will be the circuit's controlling precedent and a likely basis for certiorari review.
- **Nationwide implications**: Immigration practitioners across all circuits need to monitor this case closely, since SCOTUS may settle whether due process protections extend to detained noncitizens in removal proceedings regardless of entry method or residence duration.

## Way forward

**If you represent a detained client in the Fifth Circuit:**

- Do not file new bond-hearing motions in district court based on the panel's July 2 ruling; that precedent is now stayed and will likely be reversed.
- Monitor the Fifth Circuit docket for the date of en banc oral arguments and the eventual en banc opinion.
- Consider whether your client has standing to challenge the stay order itself or file an emergency motion for reconsideration; consult the Fifth Circuit's local rules and recent precedent on stay-order appeals.
- Prepare alternative strategies (e.g., constitutional challenges to the IIRIRA mandatory-detention scheme itself, or motions to expedite removal to shorten the detention period) that do not depend on a due-process bond-hearing right.

**If you represent detained clients outside the Fifth Circuit:**

- Note that the Fifth Circuit's forthcoming en banc opinion will be persuasive authority in other circuits; adverse rulings elsewhere may follow.
- If your circuit has not yet ruled on the due-process question, be prepared for motions practice and briefing that incorporates the Fifth Circuit's anticipated holding.
- Track Supreme Court petition activity; certiorari petitions and amicus briefs will signal the case's trajectory.
