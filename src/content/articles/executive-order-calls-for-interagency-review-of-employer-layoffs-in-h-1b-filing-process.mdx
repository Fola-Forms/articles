The executive order directs the secretaries of state, labor, and homeland security to consider whether an employer conducted layoffs within the prior year or plans future layoffs affecting similarly situated U.S. workers when adjudicating H-1B-related filings. The order signals a material shift in how federal agencies evaluate H-1B petitions and may trigger retrospective review of previously approved applications.

## What changed

The executive order, "Enhancing Program Integrity and Interagency Coordination in the Administration of the H-1B Nonimmigrant Visa Program," does not amend the Immigration and Nationality Act or the H-1B regulations. Instead, it directs the agencies to incorporate layoff and labor-market information into H-1B administration to the extent consistent with existing law and authorizes implementing rules, policies, operational guidance, or other guidance.

Specifically, employers could see additional scrutiny at multiple points in the H-1B process, including DOL Labor Condition Application (LCA) review, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) petition adjudication, consular visa issuance, and admission at the border.

Within 30 days of the September 18 order, the Labor Department's Wage and Hour Division is directed to begin reviewing data connected to earlier LCAs to determine whether further action against sponsoring employers is warranted under federal law. The review could therefore reach beyond new H-1B applications and examine information associated with earlier filings.

The executive order also calls for greater sharing of wage, employment, industry and other labour-market data among federal agencies when H-1B cases are assessed.

## Why it matters

This order expands layoff scrutiny well beyond the existing nondisplacement rules. The existing nondisplacement rules generally focus on certain layoffs during the ninety-day period before and after filing an H-1B petition supported by the LCA. The new Executive Order extends layoff consideration to all H-1B sponsoring employers as part of the adjudication analysis.

The retroactive LCA review is especially significant: the review could therefore reach beyond new H-1B applications and examine information associated with earlier filings. This means approved petitions for current H-1B workers may face renewed agency scrutiny if an employer has since conducted or announced layoffs.

The measure does not state that an employer automatically loses the ability to sponsor H-1B workers after conducting layoffs. Instead, layoffs are now a factor federal agencies must take into account when assessing H-1B cases. However, additional agency action may be needed to determine how the directive will be applied in practice.

## Way forward

- **Audit H-1B-sponsoring clients now.** If a client has conducted, announced, or plans workforce reductions in the prior 12 months, conduct a threshold analysis of which roles are "similarly situated" to the sponsored H-1B position. This information will likely be requested or reviewed by DOL and USCIS.

- **Review pending and future LCA filings.** Include narrative explanation of any recent or planned layoffs and how they do not affect the sponsored H-1B role. Anticipate heightened scrutiny during initial DOL certification.

- **Monitor agency guidance.** Additional agency action may be needed to determine how the directive will be applied in practice. USCIS and DOL will issue implementing policies and forms in coming months. Subscribe to agency announcements for updated LCA and petition instructions.

- **Document wage and market conditions.** Prepare contemporaneous documentation of prevailing wages, job duties, and labor market demand for the sponsored position. Agencies are now authorized to consider broader economic data when adjudicating cases.
