The aggravated-felony definition at [INA §101(a)(43) (8 U.S.C. §1101(a)(43))](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1101&num=0&edition=prelim) is the single most consequential statutory provision in crimmigration. A conviction that falls inside any of its 21 subparagraphs makes a noncitizen deportable under [INA §237(a)(2)(A)(iii) (8 U.S.C. §1227(a)(2)(A)(iii))](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1227&num=0&edition=prelim), permanently bars cancellation of removal under [INA §240A(a)(3)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1229b&num=0&edition=prelim), forecloses asylum under [INA §208(b)(2)(B)(i)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1158&num=0&edition=prelim), and converts a returning LPR into an arriving alien at the port of entry. The list was last meaningfully expanded by the [Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996 (IIRIRA), Pub. L. 104-208, 110 Stat. 3009-546 (Sept. 30, 1996)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/104th-congress/house-bill/3610) — the bill that bolted most of the modern categories onto the statute and made the new definitions retroactive to pre-1996 convictions.

## What changed

Before 1988 there was no "aggravated felony" category in the INA. The [Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1988](https://www.congress.gov/bill/100th-congress/house-bill/5210) created the term to capture three crimes — murder, drug trafficking, and firearms trafficking. The [Immigration Act of 1990](https://www.congress.gov/bill/101st-congress/senate-bill/358) expanded it; the [Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996 (AEDPA)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/104th-congress/senate-bill/735) added more; and [IIRIRA](https://www.congress.gov/bill/104th-congress/house-bill/3610) finished the project on September 30, 1996. The current list at [§101(a)(43)(A)–(U)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1101&num=0&edition=prelim) runs to 21 subparagraphs and covers, among other things:

- Murder, rape, sexual abuse of a minor (§101(a)(43)(A))
- Illicit trafficking in controlled substances (§101(a)(43)(B))
- Illicit trafficking in firearms or destructive devices (§101(a)(43)(C))
- Money laundering above $10,000 (§101(a)(43)(D))
- Crimes of violence as defined in [18 U.S.C. §16](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title18-section16&num=0&edition=prelim) for which the term of imprisonment is at least one year (§101(a)(43)(F))
- Theft offenses, including receipt of stolen property, for which the term of imprisonment is at least one year (§101(a)(43)(G))
- Fraud or deceit offenses where the loss to the victim exceeds $10,000 (§101(a)(43)(M)(i))
- Failure to appear to serve a sentence of at least five years (§101(a)(43)(Q))
- Obstruction of justice, perjury, or bribery of a witness for which the term of imprisonment is at least one year (§101(a)(43)(S))

**The one-year-sentence trigger.** Subparagraphs (F), (G), (N), (P), (R), (S), and (T) require a "term of imprisonment" of at least one year for the conviction to qualify. The definitional rule at [INA §101(a)(48)(B)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1101&num=0&edition=prelim) settles what counts:

> Any reference to a term of imprisonment or a sentence with respect to an offense is deemed to include the period of incarceration or confinement ordered by a court of law regardless of any suspension of the imposition or execution of that imprisonment or sentence in whole or in part.

In plain English: the sentence *imposed* triggers the aggravated-felony consequence, even if it was entirely suspended. A 365-day sentence with all 365 days suspended is still a one-year sentence for §101(a)(43)(G). A 364-day sentence is not. The single day matters.

The Supreme Court reinforced the categorical-approach analysis for the (G) theft-offense category in [*Esquivel-Quintana v. Sessions*, 137 S. Ct. 1562 (2017)](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/16pdf/16-54_5i36.pdf), and struck down the [18 U.S.C. §16(b) residual clause](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title18-section16&num=0&edition=prelim) that had powered the (F) crime-of-violence category in [*Sessions v. Dimaya*, 138 S. Ct. 1204 (2018)](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/17pdf/15-1498_1b8e.pdf). The (F) category now covers only crimes that satisfy §16(a) — the elements-based force clause.

## Why it matters

Aggravated-felony consequences are categorical and stack on top of every other ground:

- **Mandatory detention.** [INA §236(c)(1)(B)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1226&num=0&edition=prelim) requires ICE to detain a noncitizen with an aggravated-felony conviction without a bond hearing.
- **No cancellation of removal.** [§240A(a)(3) and (b)(1)(C)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1229b&num=0&edition=prelim) bar both LPR and non-LPR cancellation for any aggravated felony.
- **No asylum.** [§208(b)(2)(B)(i)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1158&num=0&edition=prelim) makes any aggravated felony a "particularly serious crime" — automatically — and bars asylum.
- **No voluntary departure.** [§240B(b)(1)(C)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1229c&num=0&edition=prelim) forecloses post-conclusion voluntary departure.
- **Permanent inadmissibility after removal.** [§212(a)(9)(A)(i) and (ii)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1182&num=0&edition=prelim) impose a 20-year (or permanent, for repeat offenders) re-entry bar.
- **Naturalization is foreclosed.** [INA §101(f)(8) and the USCIS Policy Manual on good moral character](https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-12-part-f-chapter-2) treat any aggravated felony as a permanent statutory bar to GMC, regardless of when the conviction occurred.

The one-day sentence cliff is therefore the single most important variable in many crimmigration plea negotiations. A theft conviction with a sentence of 364 days suspended is a CIMT but not necessarily an aggravated felony; the same conviction with a 365-day sentence is both — and the second outcome strips cancellation, asylum, voluntary departure, and naturalization in a single line of the judgment.

## Way forward

Practical steps for criminal-immigration counsel:

1. **Read the statute of conviction against [§101(a)(43)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1101&num=0&edition=prelim) and apply the categorical approach.** If the minimum conduct prosecutable under the statute does not match the aggravated-felony category, the conviction is not an aggravated felony for immigration purposes — even if the actual underlying facts would have qualified. *Moncrieffe v. Holder* and [*Mathis v. United States*](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/15pdf/15-6092_1qm1.pdf) control.

2. **Negotiate sentences to 364 days or less** for any offense that the one-year-sentence trigger would convert into an aggravated felony. The day saved is not academic — it preserves cancellation, asylum, and naturalization eligibility.

3. **Distinguish "term of imprisonment" from time served.** Under [§101(a)(48)(B)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1101&num=0&edition=prelim) the imposed sentence controls; a fully suspended sentence still counts. Pre-trial credit and good-time credits do not reduce the immigration-relevant figure.

4. **Watch for sentence modifications.** A nunc pro tunc state-court order reducing the sentence below the one-year threshold can take a noncitizen out of the aggravated-felony category for immigration purposes — but only if the reduction is based on a substantive defect in the original sentence, not solely to avoid immigration consequences. [*Matter of Pickering*, 23 I&N Dec. 621 (BIA 2003)](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/precedent-decisions) sets the framework.

5. **Cross-check the [USCIS Policy Manual on inadmissibility](https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-8-part-l)** for adjustment-side analysis if your client is applying for benefits while a conviction is on the record. USCIS adjudicators apply the same §101(a)(43) categorical framework.
