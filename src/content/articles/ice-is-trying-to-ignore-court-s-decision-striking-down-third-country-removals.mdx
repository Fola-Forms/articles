Immigration and Customs Enforcement officials are trying to expel at least one immigrant to a country he's never been to, despite a federal appeals court striking down the Trump administration's "third-country removal program". New court filings show the Department of Justice is advancing an aggressive—and legally dubious—argument that ICE can continue executing third-country removals until the appeals court formally issues its mandate, even though the court has already ruled the policy unlawful.

## What changed

A federal appeals court roundly rejected the Trump administration's third-country removal policy, with the three-judge panel on the First Circuit Court of Appeals ruling that immigrants must be given more time to raise fears about removal to a third country, while casting doubt on diplomatic agreements with the expanding network of "host" countries. The First Circuit decision, issued September 19, 2026, largely upheld a February 2026 district court ruling by Judge Brian E. Murphy striking down the policy.

However, in court filings seeking to transfer a Peruvian detainee from Massachusetts, the Department of Justice acknowledged the First Circuit decision but claimed that because the court "has not issued mandate," "it is ICE's position that until mandate issues, the Third Country Policy may be relied upon." A mandate is a document in which an appeals court formally notifies a lower court of the result of its decision, generally issued within a few days of a decision.

The circuit court's ruling cast doubt on the credibility of the "blanket assurances" by third countries, and noted that the policy provided no protections against chain refoulement, which occurs when ICE removes someone to a country, which then sends the immigrant to their home country, to which ICE was barred from deporting them.

## Why it matters

The DOJ's mandate-timing argument is legally questionable and creates immediate risk for detainees. It is not typically viewed as the date that a decision begins—courts typically become bound by an appellate ruling when the opinion is issued, not when the mandate formally issues days later. If ICE executes removals while relying on this aggressive interpretation, removed individuals may have claims for mandamus relief, stay of removal, or damages, but relief after removal is nearly impossible to obtain.

For practitioners, this signals that:

- The First Circuit has invalidated the entire DHS third-country removal policy, meaning immigrants with final removal orders now have stronger legal ground to resist removals to countries not designated on their original orders.
- The government will continue to litigate aggressively and may attempt removals even after appellate decisions, making emergency motions for temporary restraining orders and stays of removal critical in cases where ICE indicates a third-country removal is imminent.
- The question of when a court order becomes binding (opinion date vs. mandate date) may emerge in other circuits and could be appealed to the Supreme Court, leaving circuit-level practitioners in flux until final word.

## Way forward

- **If your client faces a third-country removal threat**: File an emergency motion for temporary restraining order / preliminary injunction in the federal court where the client is detained, citing the First Circuit's ruling. The First Circuit decision is binding in the First Circuit and highly persuasive nationwide.
- **Document the removal order**: Ensure your file includes the original immigration court's final order of removal identifying the country of removal. The First Circuit ruling turns on the fact that immigrants have due-process rights tied to that specific country, not a substitute country.
- **Monitor mandate timing**: Check the First Circuit's docket regularly for issuance of the mandate in D.V.D. v. DHS. Once issued, even DOJ's mandate argument collapses—but until then, be prepared for continued government attempts to execute removals.
- **Preserve appellate claims**: If ICE executes a removal in violation of the First Circuit ruling, document every step. Federal habeas corpus or mandamus claims may be available post-removal.
