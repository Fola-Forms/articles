A federal judge temporarily blocked a federal rule that would impose fixed admission periods of up to four years on international students and scholars, postponing the policy one day before it was set to go into effect. Judge F. Dennis Saylor IV of the US District Court in Boston issued a nationwide preliminary injunction Monday, stopping a Department of Homeland Security regulation that would have fundamentally changed how you advise F-1 and J-1 visa holders on their authorized stay in the United States.

## What changed

In mid-July, DHS announced that, going forward, foreign students and postdocs won't be allowed to stay in the U.S. for more than four years on nonimmigrant visas unless they apply for and receive an extension. The regulation would have replaced the longstanding "duration of status" system, under which foreign students may remain in the United States while enrolled in an authorised academic programme. The rule also restricted students' ability to change programmes, transfer universities or pursue another degree at the same level without approval.

U.S. District Judge F. Dennis Saylor IV granted a request to postpone the rule's effective date under the Administrative Procedure Act, but denied a request for summary judgment without prejudice, leaving the broader legal challenge unresolved. This means the injunction is preliminary—the litigation continues, but your clients' immigration status is protected in the interim.

## Why it matters

The four-year cap would have created severe practical problems for graduate and postdoctoral researchers. The rule was expected to affect doctoral students particularly severely. National Science Foundation data cited in the reports showed that doctoral programmes take a median of 5.7 years to complete. Uncertainty around immigration policy could deter international students from studying in the United States, asking whether a prospective student would "want to take a chance" on beginning a seven- or eight-year doctoral program while facing the possibility of having to leave after four years.

For now, the preliminary injunction means the ruling preserves, for now, a decades-old system that generally allows international students to remain in the United States for the duration of their academic programs. However, litigation is ongoing—the judge denied the plaintiffs' request for summary judgment, meaning DHS can continue to defend the rule at trial.

## Way forward

- **Continue normal "duration of status" planning**: Advise F-1 and J-1 clients that they may remain for the full term of their program without automatic four-year extensions, based on the preliminary injunction.
- **Maintain institutional authorization**: Counsel clients and schools to ensure ongoing SEVIS compliance and authorized-status documentation remains current.
- **Monitor the litigation**: Check for updates on the merits phase of this case; the judge denied summary judgment, so trial or additional briefing will follow.
- **Prepare contingency counsel**: If the preliminary injunction is lifted or overturned on appeal, clients with programs exceeding four years will need to file extension applications. Document all program timelines now.
