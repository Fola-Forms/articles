USCIS is updating policy guidance in the USCIS Policy Manual regarding evidentiary standards, Requests for Evidence (RFEs), and Notices of Intent to Deny (NOIDs). The policy is effective immediately for all requests pending or filed on or after August 5, 2026. This change significantly alters how you must prepare immigration benefit applications and advise clients on filing completeness.

## What changed

USCIS is updating its policy guidance to restore USCIS officers' full discretion to deny benefit requests without first issuing an RFE or NOID, as allowed by the regulations. Under this new policy, an adjudicator may deny an incomplete application directly, rather than giving the applicant or petitioner a chance to submit missing evidence first.

The burden remains on the requestor to establish that he or she is eligible for the requested benefit at the time of filing and that he or she continues to be eligible through adjudication. If the requestor meets the burden of proof to establish eligibility and, where required by statute or regulation, warrants a favorable exercise of discretion, USCIS officers approve the benefit request. In cases where the requestor does not meet the burden of proof, USCIS officers deny the benefit request.

## Why it matters

This policy dramatically shifts the risk of incomplete filings to applicants and petitioners. Previously, officers had discretion *but strong incentive* to issue an RFE before denial—giving filers a remedial opportunity. Now, an officer facing an application that lacks required initial evidence can deny it outright without any notice or chance to cure.

Practical impact:
- **Front-loaded completeness**: You cannot rely on RFEs as a safety net. Missing birth certificates, police certificates, medical exams, or other "clearly required" documents at filing may trigger immediate denial.
- **No procedural fallback**: An applicant cannot say "I'll submit it with my RFE response." The file must be complete at filing or risk denial on the merits.
- **Frivolous filings deterred**: USCIS' new policy will improve its ability to efficiently adjudicate benefit requests by discouraging frivolous, placeholder, or incomplete filings and requiring the requestor to meet his or her burden of proof by submitting complete benefit requests.

For practitioners, this means stricter filing checklists, client education on document gathering *before* filing, and potentially longer pre-filing preparation windows.

## Way forward

1. **Audit your filing checklists**: Cross-check every form's instructions against your template. Identify all "clearly required" evidence and build client deliverables around those items.

2. **Pre-file documentation**: Do not file until the applicant or petitioner has provided all initial evidence—birth certificate, police certificates, medical exam (Form I-693 where applicable), vaccination records, financial documents, etc. Treat the application as incomplete until everything is in hand.

3. **Communicate with clients early**: Explain that incomplete filings now risk immediate denial without a chance to respond. Give clients realistic timelines for document gathering before filing.

4. **Document your completeness review**: Keep a checklist or memo in the file showing that you reviewed the form instructions and verified all initial evidence was attached at filing. This creates a record that supports any appeal if denial occurs.
