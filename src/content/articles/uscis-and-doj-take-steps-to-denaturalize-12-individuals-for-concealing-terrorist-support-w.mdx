USCIS and the Department of Justice have filed denaturalization actions in U.S. district courts against 12 individuals accused of concealing serious criminal conduct, including providing material support to terrorist organizations, committing war crimes, committing espionage, and sexually abusing minors. These cases underscore the government's commitment to protecting the integrity of U.S. citizenship and national security.

## What changed

[USCIS partnered with DOJ](https://www.uscis.gov/newsroom/news-releases/uscis-and-doj-take-steps-to-denaturalize-12-individuals-for-concealing-terrorist-support-war-crimes) in filing denaturalization actions across various U.S. district courts. The 12 individuals named in these filings are accused of concealing material facts or making fraudulent statements during their citizenship applications and naturalization proceedings. Specifically, the allegations include:

- Providing material support to designated terrorist groups
- Committing war crimes
- Engaging in espionage activities
- Sexually abusing minors

Denaturalization is a rare but serious enforcement action that, if successful, reverses a person's citizenship status and can result in removal from the United States.

## Why it matters

Denaturalization cases protect the sanctity of U.S. citizenship by ensuring that individuals who obtained citizenship through fraud or material misrepresentation—particularly those involved in terrorism, war crimes, or other grave crimes—do not retain citizenship rights. When applicants conceal criminal backgrounds or ties to terrorist organizations during naturalization, they undermine the lawful immigration process and pose national security risks.

For practitioners, these cases illustrate USCIS's rigorous vetting mechanisms and DOJ's willingness to challenge citizenship grants retroactively when credible evidence of fraud emerges. The breadth of allegations—spanning terrorism, war crimes, espionage, and child abuse—signals that USCIS is scrutinizing N-400 applications and conducting post-naturalization investigations with particular attention to national security concerns.

For applicants, the takeaway is clear: any omission, misstatement, or concealment of material facts during Form N-400 proceedings carries severe consequences. Denaturalization actions can proceed years after a citizenship oath is taken.

## Way forward

- **Review N-400 disclosures carefully**: If you are preparing a naturalization application, ensure that every answer is truthful and complete. Consult an immigration attorney about any past conduct—including foreign military service, political affiliations, or involvement with organizations—that might require candid disclosure.

- **Monitor USCIS investigation trends**: Practitioners should stay informed about USCIS enforcement priorities. The agency is actively investigating post-naturalization fraud, particularly involving national security concerns.

- **Understand denaturalization grounds**: Familiarize yourself with [8 U.S.C. § 1451](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/8/1451), which governs denaturalization. Key grounds include fraud in the naturalization process and concealment of material facts.

- **Preserve documentation**: If you are a naturalized citizen and believe you have been incorrectly identified or targeted, gather all original naturalization documents, interview notes, and any evidence of truthfulness in your application. Work with an immigration attorney to prepare a defense.
