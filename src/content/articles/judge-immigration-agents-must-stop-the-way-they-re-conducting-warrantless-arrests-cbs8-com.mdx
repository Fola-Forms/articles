A federal judge in California has barred Border Patrol agents from arresting someone suspected of living in the U.S. illegally unless they have a warrant or a reason to believe the person might flee before a warrant can be obtained. This ruling, issued April 30, 2025, applies to immigration enforcement operations in the Eastern District of California and affects how Border Patrol must handle immigration arrests and removals going forward.

## What changed

U.S. District Judge Jennifer L. Thurston's ruling says Border Patrol agents can't stop people without having reasonable suspicion or return them to their countries of origin via "voluntary departure" unless that person knows their rights and agrees to leave. The decision directly addresses enforcement tactics used during an immigration sweep in the region.

The ACLU alleged border patrol agents spent nearly a week unconstitutionally detaining people who "appeared to be farmworkers or day laborers, regardless of their actual immigration status or individual circumstances." The detainees were bussed to the border, held without any way to contact family or attorneys, and coerced into signing papers that said they had waived their right to see an immigration judge and voluntarily agreed to leave the country.

Border Patrol will have to provide a report showing exactly who is detained or arrested without warrants, and why, every 60 days until the lawsuit is concluded.

## Why it matters

The ruling only applies to the Eastern District of California, but its logic addresses a fundamental Fourth Amendment question: whether Border Patrol agents need judicial warrants (signed by a judge) or administrative warrants (issued by DHS officials) to make arrests in immigration cases.

If you represent clients in removal proceedings in the Eastern District of California, this ruling creates a new defense: if your client was arrested without a judicial warrant and agents had no reasonable belief your client would flee, that arrest may be unlawful under this court's order. You can argue the arrest violated the Fourth Amendment and potentially challenge the government's authority to proceed with removal.

The coerced "voluntary departure" issue is also critical. Agents cannot use voluntary departure unless the person knows their rights and agrees to leave. If your client signed away removal rights under duress—held incommunicado, denied contact with counsel, threatened with expedited removal—this ruling supports a motion to reopen or vacate that departure agreement.

The 60-day reporting requirement also creates accountability: the government must log every warrantless arrest and explain its justification. That paper trail may help you challenge whether agents had reasonable suspicion for a stop or probable cause for an arrest.

## Way forward

- **If your client was arrested by Border Patrol in Eastern California without a judicial warrant between the dates of the underlying enforcement action and April 30, 2025**, file a motion to suppress or challenge the arrest under this ruling. Document all facts about whether agents had a warrant, reasonable suspicion, or factual grounds to believe your client would flee.

- **Request the government's 60-day detention reports** under this court's order. These may reveal patterns of warrantless arrests that undermine the government's case against your client or support a suppression motion.

- **If your client signed a voluntary departure agreement under coercion**, move to rescind it based on the court's holding that voluntary departure requires knowing and affirmative consent. Attach declarations or your client's testimony about the circumstances.

- **Watch for circuit-level appeals.** This ruling may be appealed to the Ninth Circuit. Bookmark the case and monitor whether the ruling is stayed, affirmed, or narrowed. If it's affirmed, it becomes stronger precedent for practitioners in the Ninth Circuit.
