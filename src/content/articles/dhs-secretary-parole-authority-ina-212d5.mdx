## What changed

[INA §212(d)(5)(A), codified at 8 U.S.C. §1182(d)(5)(A)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1182&num=0&edition=prelim), gives the Secretary of Homeland Security discretion to "parole into the United States temporarily under such conditions as he may prescribe only on a case-by-case basis for urgent humanitarian reasons or significant public benefit any alien applying for admission to the United States." The statute is short, the discretion it confers is wide, and the Department has, in the past four years, used that authority to launch the most consequential set of parole programs since the Indochina Refugee Resettlement of the 1970s.

The headline programs:

- [Uniting for Ukraine](https://www.uscis.gov/humanitarian/uniting-for-ukraine), announced April 21, 2022, authorizing parole for Ukrainian nationals (and their immediate family members) with a U.S.-based financial supporter.
- The [Processes for Cubans, Haitians, Nicaraguans, and Venezuelans (CHNV)](https://www.uscis.gov/CHNV), announced [January 5, 2023](https://www.dhs.gov/news/2023/01/05/dhs-continues-prepare-end-title-42-announces-new-border-enforcement-measures-and), permitting up to 30,000 individuals per month combined across the four nationalities to enter on parole with a vetted U.S. supporter.
- [Family Reunification Parole](https://www.uscis.gov/humanitarian/family-reunification-parole-process) for nationals of Colombia, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, Cuba, and Haiti with approved I-130 petitions, announced in successive [USCIS notices](https://www.federalregister.gov/agencies/u-s-citizenship-and-immigration-services) in mid-2023.
- [Military Parole in Place](https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-7-part-a-chapter-2) for spouses, parents, and children of U.S. armed forces members and veterans — formalized in [USCIS PM Volume 7, Part A, Chapter 2](https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-7-part-a-chapter-2).
- The [Keeping Families Together](https://www.uscis.gov/keepingfamiliestogether) parole-in-place process for noncitizen spouses of U.S. citizens, announced June 17, 2024.

The interpretive framework comes primarily from [USCIS Policy Manual Volume 3, Part A](https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-3-part-a) (humanitarian parole) and [Volume 3, Part B](https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-3-part-b) (significant-public-benefit parole), supplemented by the [DHS Office of the General Counsel guidance](https://www.dhs.gov/sites/default/files/2024-06/keeping_families_together_factsheet.pdf) for each program.

## Why it matters

Three structural features of §212(d)(5) drive the litigation that is now in front of the courts.

**First, "case-by-case" is doing a lot of work.** The 1996 amendments to §212(d)(5) (the [Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-104publ208/pdf/PLAW-104publ208.pdf)) tightened the statutory text from a generally available discretion to one exercisable "only on a case-by-case basis." Texas's challenge to CHNV — [Texas v. Department of Homeland Security, No. 6:23-cv-00007 (S.D. Tex.)](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.txsd.1882183/gov.uscourts.txsd.1882183.61.0.pdf) — argues that a program enrolling tens of thousands of parolees per month under a single set of eligibility criteria is, in substance, categorical rather than case-by-case. The Southern District of Texas dismissed for lack of standing on March 8, 2024; the Fifth Circuit reversed and remanded on standing in [late 2024](https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/opinions/pub/24/24-40160-CV0.pdf), keeping the substantive question alive.

**Second, parole does not confer immigration status.** A parolee is, per [INA §101(a)(13)(B)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1101&num=0&edition=prelim), not "admitted" to the United States. That matters for adjustment of status under [INA §245(a)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1255&num=0&edition=prelim), for the public-charge analysis, for [§240B voluntary departure](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1229c&num=0&edition=prelim), and for the work-authorization analysis under [8 CFR 274a.12(c)(11)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-8/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-274a/section-274a.12). Parolees apply for work authorization affirmatively on [Form I-765](https://www.uscis.gov/i-765); it is not granted automatically.

**Third, parole is revocable.** The Secretary may terminate parole "in his discretion" under [8 CFR 212.5(e)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-8/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-212/section-212.5). Parolees who built lives in the United States during the two-year parole period — bought houses, enrolled children in school, started businesses — face termination without notice-and-comment if a new administration revokes the underlying program. That risk is not theoretical; the [first Trump administration's 2018 rescission](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2018/01/22/2018-01158/notice-of-the-secretary-of-homeland-securitys-decision-to-terminate-the-temporary-protected-status) of various humanitarian protections was the proof of concept.

A subtler issue is the interaction with [parole-in-place](https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-7-part-a-chapter-2). Parole-in-place treats a noncitizen already physically in the United States as if they were paroled "into" the country — which then allows adjustment under §245(a) for a §245(a)-eligible beneficiary. The legal theory rests on the statute's reference to parole for "any alien applying for admission," read together with [Matter of Castillo-Padilla, 25 I&N Dec. 257 (BIA 2010)](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/page/file/1132446/dl). Several courts have signaled skepticism about the parole-in-place theory in the absence of statutory amendment, and the Keeping Families Together program is being litigated on exactly that ground in [State of Texas et al. v. Mayorkas et al.](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.txed.227886/gov.uscourts.txed.227886.69.0.pdf).

## Way forward

For counsel evaluating parole as a pathway for a client:

**Confirm the controlling program's current operational status.** Parole programs can be paused, capped, or terminated by USCIS notice — sometimes without formal Federal Register publication. The [USCIS humanitarian parole page](https://www.uscis.gov/humanitarian/humanitarian-or-significant-public-benefit-parole-for-individuals-outside-the-united-states) is the day-of-filing authority; do not rely on a press release from a year ago.

**Distinguish parole from refugee, asylum, and TPS routes.** Parole is a discretionary admission mechanism; it is not a humanitarian protected status. A client eligible for asylum under [INA §208](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1158&num=0&edition=prelim) or TPS under [INA §244](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1254a&num=0&edition=prelim) should pursue those statuses; parole is a complement, not a substitute. A client paroled under CHNV who develops asylum-eligible facts should file [Form I-589](https://www.uscis.gov/i-589) before the parole period expires.

**Map adjustment eligibility carefully.** A parolee who is the immediate relative of a U.S. citizen is adjustment-eligible under §245(a) if otherwise admissible — but only if the parole was lawfully granted and not terminated. Termination of parole before [Form I-485](https://www.uscis.gov/i-485) is filed can foreclose the adjustment route entirely; counsel should file as soon as eligibility crystallizes.

**Document the supporter relationship.** For CHNV and Uniting for Ukraine, the U.S.-based supporter ([Form I-134A](https://www.uscis.gov/i-134a)) is a substantive eligibility criterion, not a formality. USCIS will deny on insufficient supporter documentation. Counsel should build the I-134A package with the same rigor as an [I-864 Affidavit of Support](https://www.uscis.gov/i-864).

**Watch the litigation timeline.** The standing remand in [Texas v. DHS](https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/opinions/pub/24/24-40160-CV0.pdf) puts CHNV's substantive validity back before the district court. Any client with a parole-program-dependent strategy should have a contingency plan that does not assume the program survives intact for the full two-year parole term.

A final, practical point: at the policy level, parole authority is fungible. A future Secretary's decision to use §212(d)(5) for one purpose (say, Afghan evacuees in 2021) does not bind a successor to a different purpose. Counsel should track not only the current program landscape but also the political signals — [White House fact sheets](https://www.whitehouse.gov/briefing-room/statements-releases/), DHS press releases, and [Federal Register parole notices](https://www.federalregister.gov/) — that telegraph the next administration's posture before each individual program is announced or rescinded.

## Disclaimer

This article is editorial and educational, not legal advice. Parole programs are administered case-by-case, eligibility criteria change, and the underlying litigation is ongoing. Verify against the primary source — [INA §212(d)(5)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1182&num=0&edition=prelim), the implementing regulations at [8 CFR 212.5](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-8/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-212/section-212.5), and the operative [USCIS Policy Manual provisions](https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-3-part-a) — before relying on any specific procedural step, and consult qualified immigration counsel for matters affecting a real case.
