The Trump administration has made the path to naturalization more difficult by adding new requirements to the citizenship process, affecting lawful permanent residents seeking to complete their final step toward U.S. citizenship. If you advise clients on naturalization or immigration matters, you need to understand the specific policy changes now in effect.

## What changed

The Trump administration announced changes to the civics test that immigrants must pass to be naturalized, expanding the number of questions immigrants must be prepared to answer and increasing the number of questions they must answer correctly to pass. These changes largely revert the test to a longer and harder version introduced in 2020 during Trump's first term, but rolled back under President Biden in 2021.

Beyond the civics exam, the administration is cutting grants to groups that help prepare people to become citizens, implementing stricter social media vetting for those seeking citizenship, conducting neighborhood investigations into applicants' "moral character," and giving a more difficult civics test required for citizenship.

The Trump administration has also placed a hold on naturalization ceremonies for immigrants from 19 countries. Following a travel ban on 19 "high risk" countries including Laos and Myanmar, immigration application decisions stalled in June, and after all immigration requests from people in these countries were indefinitely halted this month, processing ground to a halt.

## Why it matters

These changes create multiple friction points in what was already a lengthy process. Your clients face a substantially harder civics exam without federally funded preparation resources—a deliberate gap. The stricter social media vetting and neighborhood character investigations expand administrative discretion and create delays.

For applicants from the 19 affected countries, the holds on naturalization ceremonies mean clients who have already passed the civics test and completed interviews cannot take the oath of allegiance. Advocates reported that in some communities they "haven't seen anyone get sworn in or get the chance to even file to become U.S. citizens for a long time".

The withdrawal of USCIS grants is consequential. In Rhode Island alone, the discontinuation jeopardized the support of four federally funded citizenship and immigration services programs. Your low-income clients who relied on these free or subsidized preparation services now face out-of-pocket costs or no assistance at all.

## Way forward

- **Assess your client's country of origin.** If they are from one of the 19 countries with the travel ban and ceremony hold (Laos, Myanmar, and others), advise them of the indefinite processing freeze. Seek updates from USCIS regularly.

- **Prepare for a harder civics exam.** Clients should study the full 100-question civics test bank (the harder 2020 version, not the streamlined Biden-era version). Do not assume the easier test pool applies.

- **Document moral character proactively.** With neighborhood investigations now standard, advise clients to gather character references from employers, community leaders, and nonprofit organizations before filing. Be aware that social media will be reviewed more intensively.

- **Budget for preparation costs.** With federal citizenship grants cut, direct clients to law school clinics, nonprofit immigration organizations, and bar-sponsored programs that may still offer free or low-cost test preparation.

- **Monitor for further changes.** USCIS may announce additional modifications to the civics test or vetting procedures. Check the Federal Register and pbs.org/newshour regularly for policy updates.
