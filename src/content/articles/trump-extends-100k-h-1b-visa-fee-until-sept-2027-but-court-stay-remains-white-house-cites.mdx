The Trump administration has extended the controversial $100,000 H-1B visa fee requirement for another 12 months, stretching it through September 21, 2027. However, a federal court order blocking fee collection remains in effect—creating a critical gap between the proclamation and actual enforcement that you need to understand before advising clients on H-1B filings.

## What changed

Trump issued a fresh presidential proclamation extending the $100,000 H-1B visa fee for another 12 months, stretching the policy through Sept 21, 2027. Under the proclamation, the restrictions imposed in September 2025 will remain in force until September 21, 2027, with H-1B petitions for workers outside the US generally required to be accompanied by a $100,000 payment, subject to limited exceptions.

The White House points to data supporting the fee's stated effectiveness: the administration cited a sharp fall in H-1B registrations by large IT outsourcing firms as evidence that the policy was having its intended effect, with their combined registrations falling from 24,946 to 2,055 — a 92 percent decline. Registrations for beneficiaries with at least a US Master's degree rose from 45.1 percent of total registrants for FY 2026 to 66.1 percent for FY 2027.

## Why it matters

**The enforcement gap is critical.** Immigration attorneys emphasize that despite the White House's aggressive push, the fee remains legally blocked by a federal court order. According to Mitch Wexler, senior counsel at Fragomen, "The US Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) remains barred from collecting the $100K H-1B fee under the Sept 2025 proclamation, which was due to expire on Sept 21, 2026. Since the new proclamation is issued as an extension, USCIS should be barred from collecting the $100K fee under the new proclamation as well."

This matters because you cannot advise clients with certainty that the fee will be collected, even though the White House intends it. A federal judge in Massachusetts struck down the government's guidance for implementing it in June, and the administration has appealed. Until an appellate court rules or the stay is lifted, USCIS has no legal authority to demand the $100,000.

The data also signals a shift in H-1B approval patterns: the Trump administration argues that the restrictions have curbed what it describes as abuse of the H-1B programme and shifted recruitment towards higher-skilled and higher-paid workers. If the fee eventually takes effect and persists, expect continued stratification toward advanced-degree holders and higher-wage petitions.

## Way forward

- **Verify current fee collection status with USCIS before filing:** The proclamation extends through September 2027, but USCIS cannot legally collect the $100,000 under the current court order. Confirm in writing—via a Request for Evidence response or pre-filing inquiry—whether USCIS is accepting the fee and on what legal basis.

- **Document salary and qualification tier:** If your client is a beneficiary or sponsoring employer, emphasize advanced degrees, specialized technical skills, and wage levels at or above the two highest wage tiers. The data show the policy is shifting approvals toward these profiles.

- **Monitor appellate litigation:** The Massachusetts federal court order is under appeal. A decision could change the fee landscape without notice. Check the <a href="https://www.cafc.uscourts.gov/">U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit</a> docket regularly or subscribe to immigration law updates.

- **Plan for cost volatility:** If you serve employer clients, discuss contingency budgeting. The fee may be collected retroactively once the court stay is lifted, or employers may face a retroactive demand for previously filed petitions. Some practitioners recommend escrow funding or holdback agreements with clients until the litigation resolves.
