A federal judge has split the difference on New York's landmark immigration enforcement restrictions. The judge blocked a New York law banning officer masks but upheld a ban on local police acting as federal immigration agents. The ruling leaves practitioners and state officials navigating two different legal regimes: the mask ban is unenforceable, but the 287(g) prohibition stands.

## What changed

A federal judge upheld a New York State law banning local police from acting as federal immigration agents while blocking a separate measure intended to prevent officers from wearing masks. Hochul signed the "Local Cops, Local Crimes Act" in May that bans state and local law enforcement agencies from helping the federal government in civil immigration enforcement cases and bans ICE agents from wearing masks during operations.

On the mask provision, the court ruled against New York. Federal prosecutors successfully argued that the state has no constitutional authority to regulate federal agencies. The logic: states cannot dictate how federal agents perform federal duties.

On the 287(g) ban—which prohibits local law enforcement from entering into federal delegation agreements to enforce immigration law—the court sided with New York. D'Agostino said it "is an appropriate means of furthering New York's legitimate interests in protecting public welfare and choosing how state and local resources are expended."

## Why it matters

For New York practitioners, the ruling is a mixed outcome. 

**On 287(g) bans:** The decision affirms that states and localities have broad authority to *opt out* of federal immigration enforcement delegations. The court upheld the ban on local immigration enforcement. "Local law enforcement should be focused on local matters, and New York taxpayers should not have to foot the bill for any collaboration with ICE." This is stable law for sanctuary jurisdictions and affects how you advise clients on local police interactions during civil immigration enforcement.

**On mask bans:** The decision narrows what states can enforce against federal agents. Immigration agents can now wear masks and conceal their identity during operations in New York without fear of state criminal penalties. This affects client safety advice and expectations around ICE enforcement visibility.

The federal government prevailed on the mask ban partly because it is now settled law. D'Agostino followed other federal courts in California, Virginia and Pennsylvania, which struck down local restrictions on face coverings. The mask ban issue is not unique to New York.

## Way forward

- **Review your local enforcement guidance.** If you represent immigrants in New York, update your client advisory materials to reflect that ICE agents may wear masks and be unidentified, but that your locality's 287(g) ban (if in effect) remains valid law.

- **Distinguish state sovereignty from federal enforcement.** The ruling confirms that states can control *how they allocate their own resources* (i.e., whether local police help ICE) but cannot regulate *how federal agents operate*. Use this in arguments about local police cooperation and consent.

- **Monitor appeal.** Hochul and James' offices "are reviewing all legal options at this time." The mask ban ruling is a preliminary injunction, not a final judgment; expect New York to appeal or retry the issue.
