Material misrepresentation under [INA §212(a)(6)(C)(i) (8 U.S.C. §1182(a)(6)(C)(i))](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1182&num=0&edition=prelim) is the sibling ground to the [false-claim-to-citizenship provision](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1182&num=0&edition=prelim), and the two are routinely confused. The difference matters: misrepresentation is waivable under [INA §212(i) (8 U.S.C. §1182(i))](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1182&num=0&edition=prelim) via [Form I-601](https://www.uscis.gov/i-601). The controlling materiality test is the Supreme Court's in [*Kungys v. United States*, 485 U.S. 759 (1988)](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/relatingtoimmigration), decided April 19, 1988, and adopted by the BIA and USCIS for §212(a)(6)(C)(i) purposes. USCIS's current guidance lives at [USCIS Policy Manual, Volume 8, Part J](https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-8-part-j).

## What changed

The statutory text bars any noncitizen who "by fraud or willfully misrepresenting a material fact, seeks to procure (or has sought to procure or has procured) a visa, other documentation, or admission into the United States or other benefit provided under this Act." The BIA reads the provision as having four elements:

1. **A misrepresentation** — an affirmative false statement, not mere silence. [*Matter of S- and B-C-*, 9 I&N Dec. 436, 448 (BIA 1961)](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/precedent-decisions).

2. **Willfulness** — the misrepresentation was deliberate and voluntary, not the product of an innocent mistake or a misunderstanding. [*Matter of Tijam*, 22 I&N Dec. 408 (BIA 1998)](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/precedent-decisions).

3. **Materiality** — the misrepresentation had a natural tendency to influence, or was capable of influencing, the agency's decision. [*Kungys v. United States*](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/relatingtoimmigration); the BIA applied *Kungys* to the immigration context in [*Matter of Tijam*](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/precedent-decisions).

4. **Made to a U.S. government official** acting in his or her official capacity, in connection with an application for an immigration benefit.

The [*Kungys* materiality test](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/relatingtoimmigration) — articulated for the denaturalization context but adopted for §212(a)(6)(C)(i) — is the "natural tendency" or "capable of influencing" standard. A misrepresentation is material if it "has a natural tendency to influence, or was capable of influencing, the decision of the decisionmaking body to which it was addressed." It does *not* have to actually have influenced the decision; the capacity to do so is sufficient. *Kungys* further explained that materiality is a question of law, not fact, that the court decides de novo.

USCIS's articulation in the [Policy Manual at Volume 8, Part J, Chapter 3](https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-8-part-j-chapter-3) sums up the modern test:

> A misrepresentation is material if it tended to shut off a line of inquiry which was relevant to the alien's eligibility, and which might well have resulted in a proper determination that he be excluded.

That is essentially the [*Matter of S- and B-C-*](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/precedent-decisions) "cut off a line of inquiry" formulation reaffirmed under *Kungys*.

## Why it matters

The misrepresentation ground is routinely deployed against:

- **Visa-application misstatements** — a denied prior application not disclosed on a [DS-160](https://ceac.state.gov/genniv/) or [DS-260](https://ceac.state.gov/iv/login.aspx); a false employment history; an undisclosed prior marriage.
- **Adjustment-application misstatements** — failure to disclose a prior arrest or removal proceeding on a [Form I-485](https://www.uscis.gov/i-485).
- **Naturalization misstatements** — a misstated period abroad on a [Form N-400](https://www.uscis.gov/n-400) or an undisclosed arrest in the GMC look-back period.
- **Visa-stamp procurement** — using a fraudulent employment letter to obtain an H-1B stamp; presenting a non-genuine educational credential.

Unlike the [false-claim-to-citizenship ground](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1182&num=0&edition=prelim), the misrepresentation ground has a waiver path. [INA §212(i)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1182&num=0&edition=prelim) authorizes the Attorney General to waive §212(a)(6)(C)(i) inadmissibility if the noncitizen is the spouse, son, or daughter of a U.S. citizen or LPR who would suffer "extreme hardship" if the noncitizen were denied admission. The hardship standard is set by [*Matter of Cervantes-Gonzalez*, 22 I&N Dec. 560 (BIA 1999)](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/precedent-decisions) and refined by [*Matter of Ngai*, 19 I&N Dec. 245 (BIA 1984)](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/precedent-decisions); USCIS's [extreme-hardship guidance lives at Volume 9, Part B, Chapter 5 of the Policy Manual](https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-9-part-b-chapter-5).

Practitioners often miss three traps:

**Innocent or negligent misstatements are not actionable.** Willfulness is a real element. A client who genuinely misremembered a date, who relied on an interpreter, or who didn't understand the question has a complete defense — and the burden is the government's. The [Policy Manual at Volume 8, Part J](https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-8-part-j) acknowledges that USCIS must prove willfulness; a credible explanation can defeat the ground entirely.

**Silence is not misrepresentation, but it can be.** Pure silence is generally not a misrepresentation — there must be an affirmative false statement. But silence in response to a *specific question* — a "no" check on a form, a head-shake at an interview — is an affirmative statement. The distinction matters at the I-9, where checking a wrong box is affirmative, but failing to volunteer ancillary information is not.

**Materiality is judged at the time of the application, not in hindsight.** A misstatement that would have shut off a relevant inquiry at the time of filing is material — even if the agency would have approved the application anyway on a full record. *Kungys* foreclosed any "but-for" defense.

## Way forward

A practical workflow:

1. **Identify the misrepresentation and the form on which it was made.** The [DS-160](https://ceac.state.gov/genniv/), [DS-260](https://ceac.state.gov/iv/login.aspx), [I-485](https://www.uscis.gov/i-485), [N-400](https://www.uscis.gov/n-400), [I-130](https://www.uscis.gov/i-130), or other application is part of the record.

2. **Apply the four-element test.** A misrepresentation, willful, material, made to a U.S. government official in connection with a benefit. If any element is missing, the ground does not apply.

3. **Test materiality under [*Kungys*](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/relatingtoimmigration).** Did the misstatement have the capacity to shut off a relevant line of inquiry? If the misstatement concerns a fact that was independently established on the record — for example, a misstated date of marriage where the marriage certificate is in the file — it may not be material.

4. **For waivable cases, prepare an [I-601 waiver](https://www.uscis.gov/i-601) under §212(i).** The qualifying-relative requirement is strict: spouse, son, or daughter of a U.S. citizen or LPR. A U.S.-citizen child does *not* qualify as a basis for the §212(i) waiver (unlike the §212(h) or §212(a)(9)(B)(v) waivers).

5. **Document hardship in the [*Cervantes-Gonzalez*](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/precedent-decisions) framework.** Health, financial, educational, country-conditions, family-separation, special-needs-of-the-relative, and country-of-relocation factors all matter. The current [USCIS extreme-hardship policy at Volume 9, Part B, Chapter 5](https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-9-part-b-chapter-5) catalogs the factors USCIS expects to see.

6. **For consular-processing clients, cross-check the [9 FAM 302.9 (DOS Foreign Affairs Manual)](https://fam.state.gov/FAM/09FAM/09FAM030209.html)** — DOS applies the same statutory text but with its own internal procedures for finding and documenting misrepresentation at the visa interview.
