President Trump has signed an executive order and renewed proclamation that add substantial compliance and attestation requirements to H-1B sponsorship, particularly for employers that have laid off or plan to reduce U.S. workforce in comparable roles. For immigration practitioners advising corporate clients on visa strategy, this represents a material shift in adjudication criteria and enforcement focus.

## What changed

Trump signed an executive order directing federal agencies to scrutinize H-1B visa applications from employers that have recently laid off, or plan to lay off, similarly situated U.S. workers. The order, signed on September 18, directs the Secretaries of State, Labor and Homeland Security to consider an employer's recent or planned layoffs when processing H-1B labor condition applications, petitions and visas.

The order directs the Departments of State, Labor and Homeland Security to consider whether a sponsoring employer "directly or indirectly" laid off employees within the previous year or plans future layoffs that "negatively affect the employment of similarly situated" American workers when adjudicating an H-1B labour condition application (LCA), petition, visa application or application for admission to the US.

The executive order directs the departments of State, Labor and Homeland Security to consult with the departments of Commerce and Education and the Small Business Administration. The agencies may share wage, employment, academic and industry information while reviewing applications for specialty-occupation workers.

Under the order, the Labor Department must begin reviewing data from previously filed labor condition applications within 30 days to determine whether enforcement action against sponsoring employers is warranted.

Separately, the proclamation renews the $100,000 fee requirement first imposed on September 19, 2025. The order extends until September 21, 2027, the $100,000 fee imposed on new H-1B visa applications.

## Why it matters

This two-pronged action substantially expands the adjudicatory scope of H-1B cases. Previously, existing law obligated high-volume H-1B employers (known as "H-1B dependent") as well as those deemed to have willfully violated H-1B program rules to attest that they have not laid off a U.S. worker and replaced them with an H-1B worker in an essentially equivalent job within 90 days before and after the filing of an H-1B petition or the placement of an H-1B worker with a secondary employer; these requirements do not apply beyond this narrow class of H-1B employers under current law.

The new order extends layoff scrutiny to **all H-1B employers**, not just those statutorily required to attest. For employers, the latest directive puts workforce decisions under greater scrutiny: applications for overseas talent will be assessed with closer attention to whether comparable US employees have recently lost their jobs or face planned layoffs.

The order does not automatically bar companies from sponsoring H-1B workers after conducting layoffs. Instead, it makes recent and planned job cuts a factor that federal agencies must consider when reviewing applications. However, this discretionary factor may result in prolonged adjudication, requests for additional evidence on business justification, or denials if agencies find nexus between layoffs and H-1B hiring.

The fact sheet said registrations from the largest information-technology outsourcing firms declined 92% after the earlier proclamation and that consular processing requests fell nearly 97%. This indicates the cumulative effect of the $100K fee and layoff scrutiny has already suppressed visa volume and may further restrict filings.

## Way forward

- **Audit workforce timing**: Before filing any new H-1B petitions, thoroughly document whether the company has conducted or is planning layoffs of similarly situated U.S. workers within the previous 12 months. If yes, prepare robust business justification that explains why the foreign worker fills a distinct role or why no U.S. workers were available after good-faith recruitment.

- **Coordinate cross-departmentally**: Ensure that Human Resources, Legal, and Finance are aligned on the company's H-1B sponsorship strategy and any planned reductions. USCIS, DOL, and DOS will increasingly coordinate wage, employment, and industry data (via Commerce, Education, and SBA inputs), so inconsistencies in public filings or internal records will be more likely to surface.

- **Review previous LCAs proactively**: The DOL's 30-day retrospective review of all previously filed labor condition applications may trigger enforcement actions or investigations against employers who sponsored H-1B workers while undergoing layoffs. Consider conducting an internal audit and, if necessary, consulting employment counsel to assess exposure under existing law (e.g., willful violations, fraud findings).

- **Plan higher budgets and longer timelines**: The $100,000 fee remains in effect through September 21, 2027 (currently blocked in court but likely to be appealed and reimposed), and new scrutiny will prolong case adjudication. Employers should budget for the fee, expect RFEs, and backload H-1B filings earlier in the fiscal year to account for adjudication delays.
