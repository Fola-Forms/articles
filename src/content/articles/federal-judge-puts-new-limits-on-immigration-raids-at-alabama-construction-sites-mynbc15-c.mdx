A federal judge in Alabama has placed new limits on how immigration officers can enter private construction sites and detain workers, a ruling that could provide additional protections for construction workers, including U.S. citizens. U.S. District Judge Jeffrey Beaverstock issued a temporary stay Monday against several DHS policies being challenged by Leo Garcia Venegas, the lead plaintiff in a class action lawsuit filed last October by the Institute for Justice, a public interest law firm.

## What changed

Under the federal judge's order, immigration officers cannot conduct warrantless raids at private construction sites. Officers also cannot detain workers without a specific reason to suspect that an individual is undocumented. The order further requires workers to be released when they provide evidence of U.S. citizenship or lawful presence.

The lawsuit was filed by Leo Garcia Venegas, who has been detained three times by ICE officers, and seeks to stop "dragnet raids" that it alleges target Latino construction workers without reasonable suspicion and continue detaining them even after they offer evidence of citizenship, violating the Fourth Amendment. The case concerns significant Fourth Amendment issues related to the Trump administration's nationwide immigration enforcement surge: whether immigration officers can enter construction sites without a warrant, whether construction employees have a reasonable expectation of privacy on the worksite, and whether officers can ignore legitimate IDs to continue an immigration stop.

## Why it matters

This ruling applies a Fourth Amendment lens to construction-site immigration enforcement that practitioners in the Southern District of Alabama (covering southern Alabama counties) must now account for in advising clients and opposing DHS enforcement. A DHS official testified in Venegas' case that REAL ID cards do not prove that someone is authorized to be in the US. The judge rejected that position, requiring DHS to honor valid government-issued documentation as proof of lawful presence or citizenship and to release workers accordingly.

Although the order is temporary and geographically limited, it establishes that courts will scrutinize warrantless, suspicion-less detention dragnets at construction sites under Fourth Amendment principles. Practitioners outside Alabama may cite this ruling in other jurisdictions if facing similar enforcement tactics, and it signals judicial receptiveness to Fourth Amendment challenges to broad ICE site raids.

## Way forward

- **If you represent construction workers or construction companies in southern Alabama**: Brief them on the new DHS restrictions. Workers should carry valid government ID (REAL ID, passport, etc.) and know they have the right to demand release upon showing it.
- **If you are litigating similar enforcement tactics elsewhere**: Monitor this case as it proceeds on the merits (it is not yet a final ruling, only a temporary stay). Use the court's reasoning in your own Fourth Amendment challenges.
- **If you receive notice of a construction-site ICE raid in the affected counties**: Consider contacting the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of Alabama and cite the Beaverstock order to challenge the legality of warrantless entry or detention without particularized suspicion.
- **Verify the geographic scope**: Confirm which specific counties in southern Alabama are covered by this order; the stay applies only within the jurisdiction of that federal district court.
