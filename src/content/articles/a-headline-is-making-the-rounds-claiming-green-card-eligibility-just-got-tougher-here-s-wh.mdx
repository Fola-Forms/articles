On May 21, 2026, USCIS released PM-602-0199, titled "Adjustment of Status is a Matter of Discretion and Administrative Grace, and an Extraordinary Relief that Permits Applicants to Dispense with the Ordinary Consular Visa Process." The announcement sparked concern about tighter green card access, but a key point got lost in the noise: the underlying eligibility rules did not change. What shifted is the evidentiary burden—and that distinction matters for how you prepare your client's case.

## What changed

PM-602-0199 did not repeal any statute, did not eliminate any green card category, and did not change who is technically eligible to file an I-485. What it did do is formally reframe the standard that USCIS officers must apply when reviewing those applications.

Previously, officers would generally approve applications where the applicant met the legal requirements. Under PM-602-0199, officers are now expected to take a deeper look and may deny applications even when eligibility is technically met, if negative factors outweigh the positive ones.

The memo divides an I-485 file into two distinct layers:

1. **The eligibility packet**: forms, identity documents, medical exam, immigrant petition evidence, I-94, status documents, and civil records—the same as before.
2. **The discretionary packet**: evidence showing why USCIS should choose to exercise favorable discretion. A strong discretionary packet may include a short legal cover letter, an immigration-status chronology, proof of timely filings and extensions, tax records, W-2s or pay records where relevant, evidence of lawful work authorization, family-equity evidence, community service records, medical or humanitarian evidence, and a direct explanation of any negative factor.

## Why it matters

Documentation matters more than it used to. A thin file that technically qualifies is now more likely to draw a Request for Evidence or an in-person interview than it was a couple of years ago.

The impact varies by applicant profile:

- **Higher risk**: Applicants who have unlawful presence or overstays (even brief); unauthorized employment; status gaps or reinstatements; parole-based AOS pathways; entry without inspection; prior fraud or misrepresentation; criminal history; or situations where the applicant clearly stayed in the U.S. to avoid consular processing.

- **Lower risk but not immune**: H-1B, L-1, and O-1 holders with continuous lawful status and authorized employment; EB-1, EB-2 NIW, and other employment-based applicants with strong approved petitions; immediate relatives of U.S. citizens with clean histories; applicants with no status violations, no unauthorized work, and no adverse immigration or criminal record.

Every active I-485 in your docket is now subject to a higher discretionary bar—retroactively. This applies to pending cases, not just new filings.

## Way forward

- **Audit your pending I-485s immediately.** Identify which cases have thin discretionary narratives and prepare supplemental evidence packages now, before RFEs arrive.

- **Reframe the narrative for each case.** Treat the adjustment application as a documented legal filing, not a routine form package, and affirmatively present the favorable discretionary factors in the case.

- **Build the discretionary packet from day one.** Families with pending I-485 applications should ensure their files include strong, well-documented proof of the bona fide nature of the relationship and the applicant's clean immigration history.

- **Evaluate the consular processing option.** PM-602-0199 calls for a more carefully prepared and fully documented application, and a clear-eyed assessment of the alternative of consular processing.
